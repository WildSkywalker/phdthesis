\section{t-SNE and Dimension Reduction} \label{intro_chap:sec:tsne}
\subsection{The Ubiquity and Challenge of High-Dimensional Data} \label{intro_chap:sec:tsne:ubiquity}
Most data and data sets we deal with on a daily basis are multi-dimensional by
nature. For example, a simple class roster can have multiple exam and homework
scores. If the total number of assignments is greater than 3, then this single
class roster is already beyond what we can natively visualize (if we want to
represent each student as a point). On the other hand, each image can be seen
as a high-dimensional vector and the dimensionalities increase rapidly while
the resolution we are able to capture advances with technology. The number of
variables scale in a breakneck pace as we are more capable of capturing
detail-rich data as well as automating the process (\cite{Donoho_Dimensions}).
As stated in the last section, this process gives us practically unlimited
potentials. The objects and processes we would like to study were recorded with
utmost details, unlike the past when vital bits of information could be lost
forever. However, this also brings caveats and new mathematical challenges.

One of the most important and hardest challenges is the noise that comes with
data. Apart from noise caused by engineering factors, the growth of
dimensionality also brings noise because many dimensions may be irrelevant for
the problems one want to solve. The extra dimensions themselves become the
``noise". In 1996, Robert Tibshirani discovered the famous Least Absolute
Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) for the purpose of variable selection
(\cite{lasso1996}). Consider a sample consisting of $N$ cases, each with
dimension $p$. Let $y_i$ be the outcome and $x_i$ be the $p$-dimensional vector
that represents the $i$-th case. LASSO aims to solve
\begin{align}
    \min_{\beta_0,\beta} \sum_{i=1}^N (y_i - \beta_0 - x_i^T\beta)^2,
\end{align}
with constraint
\begin{align}
    \sum_{j=1}^p |\beta_j| \leq t.
\end{align}
LASSO marks the early realization and effort mathematicians and scientists were trying to mitigate the overwhelming information brought by high dimensional data. LASSO was also closely related to Basis Pursuit Denoising (BPDN), introduced in 1994 (\cite{BPDN}). BPDN is a well known NP-hard problem. Before the age of big data comes, mathematicians had been thinking about extracting useful signals from noise/spurious correlations. After the dawn of 21st century and the explosive growth of computer science, problems like BPDN and LASSO become even more important after we add computational cost to the equation (\cite{variableselection2010}).

Apart from feature and variable selection, people also seek to ``compress" the
data, creating a mathematical summary of the essence. t-SNE is one of such
examples. Instead of making a selection, t-SNE aims to find a low-dimensional
representation of the data and taking the entire data set into account. Another
good example is the Variational Auto Encoder (\cite{kingma2013auto}), a
generative model that is able to scale well with large data sets using a
low-dimensional ``latent space". We will discuss VAE and our related work on
this topic in chapter 4.

In the face of large amount of high dimensional data, digesting it ``as is'' is
more often than not impossible. The above examples show us that we either make
a selection, or we perform compression, which are the two most common
approaches in terms of getting what we need from the data. We would like to
discuss this further because high-dimensional data is not only mathematically
and computationally hard to deal with, but also tends to mask the insights we
are looking for. This is somehow a paradoxical nature of high-dimensionality.
Faithful recording of data assures us the information and values we seek are
within the data sets and within in our reach. But the form and scaling of the
high-dimensional data also buries the light we are after. This is the so-called
``curse of dimensionality".

The term ``curse of dimensionality" was first coined by Richard Bellman in 1961
(\cite{Bellman1961adaptive}). It refers to various phenomena that appears when
analyzing data in high-dimensional space that do not occur when dimensionality
is low. Qualitatively speaking, the ``curse'' usually means that as
dimensionality increases, the exponential increase of volume makes the same
amount of data sparse and harder to work with.

To understand this concept, let us first look at the famous example of
hypercube vs hypersphere. Consider a hypercube with edge length $2r$ in
$d$-dimensional Euclidean space and its inscribed sphere with radius $r$.
Denote $\Gamma$ the gamma function and $C_d$, $S_d$ the volume of the hypercube
and hypersphere respectively. Then we have
\begin{align}
    \lim_{d \to \infty} \frac{S_d}{C_d} = \lim_{d \to \infty} \frac{\pi^{d/2}}{d2^{d-1}\Gamma(d/2)} = 0.
\end{align}
This means that our low-dimensional intuition fails. The volume of hypercubes are mostly ``concentrated" in the corners and the inscribed sphere's volume becomes negligible. If we want to analyze the data related to the hypercube, a data set that is uniformly distributed in the hypercube will become an increasingly small part of the whole space. In general, as we increase the dimension of our sample space, any finite data set will occupy a vanishingly small part of it. On the other hand, to maintain the roughly same density, one needs to accumulate exponentially more data points to compensate for the increase in dimensions.

Such fact also induces a data mining problem, where it is unrealistic to gather
enough amount of data points to guarantee the separability of data generated by
two different distributions. It is a well known fact in machine learning that
too many features with inadequate data points will lead to overfitting. This
discovery is not new, either. In 1968, Hughes showed that adding more features
past certain points without providing more data points will cause worse
classification performance (\cite{hughes1968}). Later it becomes known as the
famous Hughes phenomenon. Adding features is not a free lunch and the results
could be detrimental.

To further illustrate the curse of dimensionality, let us look at two extra
examples, one mathematical and one from the field of biology. We just compared
the volume of hypercubes and hyperspheres as the dimensionality tends to
infinity. This comparison is closely related to another phenomenon called the
concentration of distance. It refers to the fact that uniformly sampled points
in high-dimensional space tend to be equidistant from each other. This can be
seen more intuitively from the two collections of plots below.

For the second plot, we replace the unit cube with the unit sphere as our

concentrated near the surface, and the volume of a hypercube is mostly

The above two plots reinforce the intuition behind the data mining problem we
just brought up. Distance, one of the most fundamental and important feature
that distinguishes data points, becomes less and less effective as the
dimension of the data increases. It directly leads to the fact that purely
distance-based algorithms will perform poorly with high-dimensional data sets.
It is crucial to find ways to extract the information we need from high
dimensions, then compress, analyze and visualize it in lower-dimensions. The
concentration of distance is also one of the reasons that algorithms such as
t-SNE are invented. As we will see in the coming sections, t-SNE encodes the
neighborhood information using probability distributions, rather than plain
Euclidean distance.

The last example we would like to share is the RNA sequencing and many genome
projects, where the curse of dimensionality manifests itself in real-world
scientific research. Generally speaking, RNA sequencing experiments measure the
abundance of tens of thousands of different RNA transcripts on expression
level. Such rich information can exist simultaneously within a single
biological sample because life itself is tremendously complex. From a data
science point of view, all these genes/transcripts are features and each acts
as a dimension so the dimensionality is extremely high. For humans, that number
is somewhere roughly between $10^4$ to $10^5$. For Genome sequencing that
number can be even larger, ranging from $10^6$ to $10^7$.

This causes all kinds of problems we mentioned about high-dimensional data
(\cite{StegleRNA2015}, \cite{KiselevRNA2019}, \cite{Visscher2017}). On one
hand, Euclidean distance becomes less meaningful. The Euclidean distance
between two patients (data points) may not contain any useful implications for
their similarities in genetic traits or tendencies for a specific disease. The
occurrence of spurious correlations are much higher, that people often finds
significant correlations between features(genes) and outcomes(disease status)
that are purely coincidental and not biologically meaningful. On the other
hand, Hughes phenomenon is prevalent in model training because it is nearly
impossible to gather enough data so that the sample size is significantly
larger than the number of features. This results in massive overfitting and
poor predictions for critical problems such as tumor types classification. At
the same time, genetic sequences are absolute nightmares for hypothesis testing
due to their sheer complexity, making false positives much more prone to
happen.

For these very reasons, dimension reduction really shines through the field of
biology and bioinformatics. Most algorithms introduced in the next section are
still widely used today, from the old classic PCA, to methods created in 21st
century such as t-SNE. Dimension reduction is a crucial toolset that makes
continuing research and analysis of genetic data possible, with its impact and
usage expanding well beyond the field of biology to almost all scientific
subjects related to data science, statistics and machine learning.

With all the above being said, it is of paramount importance to study dimension
reduction techniques and algorithms. The knowledge of which aspects of data are
important is obviously not a priori, so it is impossible to select the
features/dimensions we need beforehand. The only option for us is to develop
the mathematical theory, and reduce the dimensionality to the extent that the
information we seek becomes apparent. Now we are almost a quarter into the 21st
century, the development of technology resonates with the need for dimension
reduction, and puts our mathematical legacy to the test.
\subsection{A Brief History of Dimensionality Reduction} \label{intro_chap:sec:tsne:DR}
The theory of dimension reduction can be traced back to the beginning of 20th
century. In 1901, Karl Pearson introduced the geometric idea of finding lines
and planes of best fit to data points, later known as the famous Principle
Component Analysis (PCA)(\cite{Pearson1901PCA}). This technique was also
independently discovered by Harold Hotelling in 1933 (\cite{Hotelling1933}).
The idea behind PCA is mainly linear algebra. It first centers the data set at
zero and then tries to find the largest eigenvalue of the covariance matrix.
The corresponding eigenvector marks the direction of the most variance in the
data, making it the biggest principle component. Then this component gets
subtracted and the algorithm repeats to find the next largest eigenvalue. The
algorithm can stop at any number of principle component. The corresponding
eigenvalues and eigenvectors will then be used to construct a subspace with
lower dimensions that represents the original data.

Intuitively speaking, PCA tries to fit a high-dimensional ellipsoid to the data
set. Each axis of the ellipsoid represents one principle component. The larger
the eigenvalue, the longer the corresponding axis of the ellipsoid. PCA is
simple and elegant, so much so that it is still widely used today, but its
shortcomings are also apparent. PCA assumes linearity as what it does is
primarily finding a new coordinate system where the axes, a.k.a. the principle
components, are linear combinations of the original variables. The mapping from
the original high-dimensional space to the lower-dimensional subspace defined
by principle components is an orthogonal linear transformation. This means that
by analyzing the covariance matrix, PCA can mostly only capture linear
dependencies. This approach will fail if the intrinsic structure of the data is
non-linear. For example, PCA is likely to give two principle components if
asked to analyze a circle, a typical 1-dimensional manifold in 2-dimensional
Euclidean space. It is unable to see that the points on a circle are all part
of a 1-dimensional space. Similar results with Swiss roll, PCA will very likely
be unable to ``unroll" the Swiss roll into its natural 2-dimensional
representation, but rather cut through it.

PCA is one of the most important and widely used dimension reduction
algorithms. Though its assumption on linearity limits its application, it also
allows it to be computationally efficient using modern hardware and
parallelism. It outperforms many modern algorithms in terms of speed and
scalability. For this reason PCA is included in many modern software packages
like scikit-learn and PyTorch.

Continuing onward, in the 1950s, Warren Torgerson invented Multidimensional
Scaling (MDS) (\cite{Torgerson1952}, \cite{torgerson1958theory}), with the
classical version often known as Torgerson Scaling. MDS is one of the early
non-linear dimension reduction algorithms. Given a distance/dissimilarity
matrix (where the distance does not have to be Euclidean distance) and a target
dimension $N$, MDS aims to find a set of points in $N$-dimensional space where
the pairwise distances are preserved as much as possible. Often one lets $N=2$
so the result can be easily visualized. In classical MDS, the final goal is to
minimize the loss function, which is also called strain:
\begin{align} \label{strain_def}
    \text{Strain}(x_1,\dots,x_n) = \left(\frac{\sum_{i,j}(b_{ij}-x^T_i x_j)^2}{\sum_{i,j}b^2_{i,j}}\right).
\end{align}
Here, $x_i$'s are vectors in $N$-dimensional space that we are trying to find, and $b_{ij}$ are the entries from the Gram matrix that can be computed using the input data. Compared to PCA, MDS no longer assumes linear relationships between data points, and is more intuitive as the distance between points is often a good reflection of their similarity. One of the most famous applications of MDS is the color wheel. One might never hear about MDS, but they must have used the color wheel at some point of their lives.

    \caption{The famous 2-dimensional color wheel, created by MDS. It effectively and intuitively captured the similarity and discrepancies between different colors on the spectrum of visible light.}%
    \label{fig:color_wheel}
\end{figure}

Being intuitive and flexible, MDS gained a lot of popularity and it was
developed into a family of algorithms. Shepard and Kruskal developed non-metric
MDS independently in 1962 and 1964 (\cite{Shepard_1962},\cite{Kruskal_1964}).
Their work allowed MDS to handle Ordinal data, meaning the modified algorithm
only requires the rank order of dissimilarities. Such outstanding flexibility
is one of the major reasons that it is still widely used today, especially when
there is a lack of good distance metrics or quantitative data, such as market
research. However, MDS is not without drawbacks. The axes of the output do not
have inherent meanings from the original data set and sometimes it is not
possible to find a solution with low enough stress values (the stain in the
classical MDS). More importantly, MDS is not computationally efficient. Though
modern algorithms like t-SNE and UMAP share some of the disadvantages with the
MDS family, they often outperform MDS by a fair margin. Nevertheless, the
limitations of MDS algorithms do not diminish their importance. MDS is still
one of the most important algorithms in the field of dimension reduction.

Fast forwarding to the millennium, the rapid advancement of computer science
and applied mathematics created increasingly stronger needs to study data of
nonlinear nature. Scientists had been striving to create new tools and
algorithms that can handle the emerging complex data sets.Though mentioned in
earlier works, many regarded the work of Roweis and Saul in 2000 as the pillar
of manifold learning (\cite{Roweis2000}). Unlike PCA, which tries to project a
collection of high dimensional vectors onto a linear subspace, manifold
learning projects the high-dimensional data onto a lower-dimensional latent
manifold. Since manifold learning was created specifically to tackle problems
with nonlinear nature, it is also called Nonlinear Dimension Reduction (NLDR).

In the same paper, Roweis and Saul presented the Local Linear Embedding (LLE),
one of the classic algorithms in NLDR. From this point onward, readers who are
familiar with SNE and t-SNE may notice the resemblance between the algorithms
and how they draw inspiration from each other. Given original data points $X_i
    \in \mathbb{R}^D$, the goal of LLE is to find a set of points $Y_i \in
    \mathbb{R}^d$ usually with $D >> d$ such that $Y_i$'s form a good
representation(embedding) of $X_i$'s. This is done in two steps. First, LLE
looks at each point $X_i$ and tries to find the closest approximation of $X_i$
using the linear combination of other points. i.e., for each $i$, LLE computes
\begin{align}
    \norm{X_i - \sum_{j} W_{ij}X_j}^2,
\end{align}
where $W_{ij}$ are the weight we assigned to each $X_j$. $W_{ij}$ is nonzero when $X_j$ is within a certain distance of $X_i$, or when $X_j$ is considered a ``neighbor". This distance is a hyperparameter set by the user. Summing up through all $i$'s gives us the reconstruction error:
\begin{align}
    E(W) := \sum_{i=1}^N \norm{X_i - \sum_{j} W_{ij}X_j}^2.
\end{align}
While LLE aims to minimize $E(W)$, it is also subject to the restriction
\begin{align}
    \sum_{j} W_{ij} = 1.
\end{align}
This ensures that $W$ is not affected by rotation and translation. The first step is finished when LLE successfully finds the weight that minimizes $E(W)$. This is a typical constraint least square problem so we are able to find the global minimum. Now the weight $W_{ij}$'s are fixed and the second step begins. LLE now tries to find output $Y_i$'s that minimize the following cost function:
\begin{align}
    C(Y) := \sum_{i=1}^N \norm{Y_i - \sum_{j} W_{ij}Y_j}^2.
\end{align}
The algorithm terminates when the minima of $C(Y)$ is found. Directly from the same paper (\cite{Roweis2000}), we know that finding the minimum of $C(Y)$ is equivalent to solving a sparse eigenvalue problem and we are able to find the global minimum. This is one of the most appealing mathematical properties of LLE, that both of its core steps yield globally optimal solutions. However, nothing is perfect and LLE does come with its own shortcomings. Let us discuss that after we introduce another NLDR algorithm that was created around the same time, and is just as famous and important: Isomap.

Isomap was first introduced in around 2000 (\cite{tenenbaum_global_2000}). It
is a modern generalization of MDS. Instead of using the Euclidean distance,
Isomap generates the so called geodesic distance between each pair data points
then apply MDS to get the low-dimensional output. We start with our
high-dimensional input $X_i \in \mathbb{R}^D$. The first step of Isomap is to
find the neighbors of each $X_i$. This can be achieved by either considering a
ball with radius $\epsilon$ around $X_i$ and see which points are inside the
ball, or by using the $k$ nearest neighbors. Both $\epsilon$ and $k$ are
hyperparameters set by the user.

After getting the neighbors for each point, we will construct a weighted graph
$G$ based on the information we just obtained. Each vertex of $G$ represents an
input data point. We will use the term vertex $i$ when the vertex represents
$X_i$. We draw an edge between vertex $i$ and vertex $j$ if $X_j$ is a neighbor
of $X_i$, which is determined during the previous step of the algorithm. The
weight of the edge $(i,j)$ is the Euclidean distance between $X_i$ and $X_j$.
Next up, we perform the Dijkstra algorithm on $G$, which yields the shortest
graph distance between each pair of vertices $i$ and $j$. This distance is
called the geodesic distance, denoted by $d_G(i,j)$. This concludes the second
step of Isomap.

Afterwards, since $d_G(i,j)$ is well defined for each tuple $(i,j)$, we have a
distance matrix as the input of the MDS algorithm. Running the algorithm with
the target low dimension $d$ will produce the final result of Isomap.

    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=14cm]{isomap.jpg}
    \caption{The input Swiss roll (left) and the output of Isomap (right). The Isomap successfully captures the nature of the manifold and ``unrolls" the Swiss roll.}%
    \label{fig:isomap_swissroll}
\end{figure}

With the introduction of Isomap, it is easier to explain the drawbacks of LLE.
As we can see in the steps of LLE, it emphasizes the local reconstruction of
each data point using its neighbors. Because of this, LLE can potentially fail
to preserve the global structure of the data, namely the distances on a larger
scale. Points that are very far away in the original data set can be mapped
close to one another in the output of LLE, especially when the space itself is
very ``curved". This shortcoming is addressed by Isomap, as the intermediate
step of finding the graph $G$ is specifically designed to preserve global
structure. Using MDS subsequently ensures that such preservation is translated
into the low-dimensional output. The graph $G$ represents the grand picture
Isomap tries to summarize.
\begin{figure}[ht]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=10cm]{LLE_failed.jpg}
    \caption{A failed LLE output based on the same Swiss roll input in figure \ref{fig:isomap_swissroll}. The algorithm tries to search for neighbors in a overly broad area, causing the result to be distorted.}%
    \label{fig:lle_swissroll_failed}
\end{figure}
Nevertheless, Isomap is not perfect, either. As we already mentioned earlier in this section, MDS is a computationally expensive algorithm. On top of that, given a graph $G = (V,E)$, the Dijkstra algorithm has a time complexity of $O(|E| + |V| \log |V|)$, where $|E|$ and $|V|$ are the number of edges and vertices in the graph respectively (\cite{Fredman1984fibonacci}). For dense graphs, $|E|$ will scale quadratically with $|V|$. By combining two computationally intense algorithms, Isomap is inevitably slow and becomes a bad choice for large and dense data sets.

Moreover, Isomap is very sensitive to the choice of hyperparameters and
outliers. If the number of neighbors or the ball radius is too small, Isomap
will sometimes produce a disconnected graph, prevents the algorithm from
generating a global structure. But if the hyperparameters are too large, the
algorithm might include ``short-circuit" edges that connect points across gaps
or folds in the manifold that are close in the ambient Euclidean space, such as
distant points on a tightly packed Swiss roll. Then in this case Isomap will
contain the worst of both worlds, by being an extraordinarily slow algorithm
that also carries the disadvantages of LLE. Besides that, since Isomap is
designed to preserve the global structure, it will cover everything, including
outliers. Having an outlier in the data set, such as a point far away from
everything else, can significantly disrupt the output.

Finally, Isomap carries some implicit assumptions. It assumes that the data on
the manifold is well sampled, meaning that the graph constructed in Isomap is a
truthful representation of the true geodesic distances on the manifold, from a
differential geometry perspective. It also assumes that the intrinsic geometry
of the manifold can be isometrically embedded into a lower-dimensional
Euclidean space, hence the name. Such assumptions are not always true, and we
should treat the output with caution if the stress values (similar to the
definition in \eqref{strain_def}) are high.

Both LLE and Isomap are important milestones in the advancement of dimension
reduction algorithms. With different focusing aspects, both provided us with a
useful set of tools to analyze high-dimensional data, suited for different
scenarios. However, mathematicians and computer scientists did not stop there.
All the algorithms up to this point still work primarily with Euclidean
distance. What if we want to extract the abstract structure of similarity out
of the data sets, rather than the distance itself? What if we encode the
information of neighborhood using probability distribution, rather than plain
physical proximity? Such ideas inspired the creation of stochastic neighbor
embedding (SNE) and the subsequent t-stochastic neighbor embedding (t-SNE). And
that is where our story begins.
\subsection{The Road to t-SNE} \label{intro_chap:sec:tsne:preintro}
SNE was first introduced in 2002 by Hinton and Roweis
(\cite{NIPS2002_6150ccc6}, with Roweis also being the creator of LLE). Like we
just mentioned at the end of the previous section, SNE uses probability
distributions to record the information of neighborhood. With this new
framework, we need a new concept to measure the distance between two
probability distributions, and that is the famous Kullback-Leibler (KL)
divergence.
\begin{definition} \label{def:KL_divergence}
    Let $P$ and $Q$ be two probability measures with $p(x)$ and $q(x)$ as their respective probability density functions. The KL divergence between $P$ and $Q$ is defined as:
    \begin{align}
        D_{KL}(P||Q) = \int p(x) \log\left(\frac{p(x)}{q(x)}\right) dx.
    \end{align}
    In particular, if $P$ and $Q$ are discrete probability measures(distributions) defined on the same sample space $\mathcal{X}$, then the KL divergence is defined as:
    \begin{align}
        D_{KL}(P||Q) = \sum_{x \in \mathcal{X}} p(x) \log\left(\frac{p(x)}{q(x)}\right).
    \end{align}
\end{definition}
Note that the KL divergence is zero if and only if $P$ and $Q$ are the same distribution. At the same time, it is not symmetric and is not a true distance metric. It is also called the relative entropy. Since both SNE and t-SNE will construct probability distributions from the original data set, rather than plain Euclidean distance, KL divergence is used to formulate the loss function we aim to minimize.

SNE starts with defining the following probability mass function for each data
point $X_i$: for each $j \neq i$, let
\begin{align}\label{SNE_input_kernel}
    p_{j|i} = \frac{\exp(-\norm{X_i - X_j}^2/2\sigma_i^2)}{\sum_{k \neq i}\exp(-\norm{X_i - X_k}^2/2\sigma_i^2)}.
\end{align}
$\sigma_i$ is a hyperparameter that is set by the user or by computation. We restrict that for each $i$, the induced probability distribution has the same entropy for all $i$. This means for some constant $\rho$, we have
\begin{align} \label{SNE_input_entropy}
    \sum_{j \neq i} p_{j|i} \log\left(p_{j|i}\right) = \log(N\rho),
\end{align}
where $N$ is the number of data points. The quantity $N\rho := \text{Perp}$ is called perplexity. This is a very clever design and it serves as a ``slide bar" for each $X_i$. For example, the majority of the data points are located inside a ball with radius 1 and there is one data point that is more than 1000 units away. Then from that point's perspective, the rest of the data points are roughly "equally" distant from itself. The induced probability distribution will be very close to a discrete uniform distribution. A lot of information is lost during this process. By introducing perplexity, we allow each induced probability distribution to zoom in and out so that they are "observing" the rest from the data set on the same scale. Later we will see that this setting is not only backed up by clear intuition, but also plays an important role in proving the convergence to equilibrium measure in chapter 2, as well as in the prior work by Auffinger and Fletcher (\cite{auffinger-tsne}).

Let $Y_i$ be the low-dimensional output of SNE. We can define a similar
probability mass function:
\begin{align} \label{SNE_output_kernel}
    q_{i|j} = \frac{\exp(-\norm{Y_i - Y_j}^2)}{\sum_{k \neq i}\exp(-\norm{Y_i - Y_k}^2)}.
\end{align}
Next, SNE will then try to minimize the KL divergence between the two (collections of) distributions, i.e.:
\begin{align} \label{SNE_loss_function}
    L = \sum_{i=1}^N D_{KL}(P_i||Q_i) = \sum_{i=1}^N \sum_{j \neq i} p_{j|i} \log\left(\frac{p_{j|i}}{q_{j|i}}\right).
\end{align}
Since the input $X_i$'s are given and fixed, the loss function $L$ is a function of the output points $Y_i$'s. The minimum can be found using gradient descent. Different from LLE, SNE is not guaranteed to find a global minimum.

Though the formulation of SNE resembles LLE, the invention of SNE was still a
refreshing evolution. From plain distance, to geodesic distance from
constructed graphs, and now to probability distribution, it is good to see that
mathematicians are using more flexible concepts to encode the general landscape
of data. But people soon discovered that SNE has its own problems. Since the
output distribution decays exponentially and it does not have a slide bar like
perplexity, SNE is not able to distinguish clusters that are not in close
proximity. This is the well-known ``crowding problem" of SNE, and it directly
inspired the birth the subsequent t-SNE algorithm (\cite{key}).

The t-SNE algorithm shares the same procedure as SNE. However, instead of using
the Gaussian function in the output schema, t-SNE uses the much slower decaying
student's t-distribution with 1 degree of freedom, i.e.,
\begin{align} \label{tSNE_output_kernel}
    q_{ij} = \frac{(1 + \norm{Y_i - Y_j}^2)^{-1}}{\sum_{k \neq i}(1 + \norm{Y_i - Y_k}^2)^{-1}}.
\end{align}
That is also where the letter ``t" in the name comes from. The t-distribution greatly mitigates the crowding problem, and it is able to perform very well across different data sets. Also, t-SNE defines the input probability mass $p_{ij}$ as
\begin{align}
    p_{ij} = \frac{p_{j|i}+p_{i|j}}{2n},
\end{align}
which makes the input probability masses symmetric. One of the greatest and most well-known showcase of t-SNE is its implementation on the MNIST data set. The Modified National Institute of Standards and Technology (MNIST) is a large database consisting of handwritten digits. It is one of the mostly commonly used public data set for machine learning and computer vision. Started with USPS database that records handwritten zip codes, today's MNIST contains 60000 training images and 10000 testing images and serves as a benchmark for many machine learning algorithms. t-SNE is able to effectively capture the structure of data and clearly cluster different digits into separate groups. This algorithm is one of the greatest achievements in the field of dimension reduction and unsupervised learning in the 21st century.
\begin{figure}[ht]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=14cm]{mnist_tsne.jpg}
    \caption{A sample of the MNIST dataset (left) and the output of t-SNE (right). t-SNE successfully grouped the handwriting with different digits together, with some minor imperfections (there are two clusters for the digit 0).}%
    \label{fig:mnist_tsne}
\end{figure}
Unfortunately, we need to repeat the same statement again: nothing is perfect. t-SNE is also a computationally expensive algorithm. If the data set is large and complex, it is advised to pre-process the data set using lightweight dimension reductions such as PCA. Meanwhile, though with better flexibility and overall performance, t-SNE inherits the same drawback from LLE: poor performance at preserving global structures. The distance between two clusters are often meaningless. We only know that the corresponding original data points do not belong to the same group, but little information can be extracted by the distance between the resulting low-dimensional clusters. t-SNE is also non-deterministic by nature: ending results might vary slightly for the same data set and hyperparameters. While the general cluster structures might be similar, the exact positions and orientations can vary, which can be sometimes confusing.

Such traits of t-SNE inspired some discussion regarding the mathematical nature
of the algorithm. First, if t-SNE is non-deterministic, can we say something
about its convergence and asymptotic behavior? Will the output of t-SNE
converge to a certain equilibrium measure as we increase the number of samples
to infinity? The answer is yes. We have the following results from
\cite{auffinger-tsne}:

\begin{theorem} [Theorem 1.1, \cite{auffinger-tsne}] \label{thm:auffinger-tsne_1.1}
    Assume all input data points of t-SNE are drawn independent from a probability measure $\mu_X$. $\mu_X$ has a $C^1$ density function $f(x)$ and compact support. Denote $X$, $Y$ the input and output data set, $n$ the number of data points, $\rho$ the perplexity, and $L_{n,\rho}(X,Y)$ the loss function (KL-divergence) of t-SNE.

    Then for any $\rho \in (0,1)$ given, there exists a function $I_{\rho}$ and a
    set of measures $\mathcal{P}_{\mu_X}$, such that
    \begin{align}
        \lim_{n \to \infty} \inf_{Y} L_{n,\rho}(X,Y) = \inf_{\mu \in \mathcal{P}_{\mu_X}} I_{\rho}(\mu).
    \end{align}
\end{theorem}
If we write
\begin{align}
    \mu_n = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \delta_{X_i,Y_i}
\end{align}
as the empirical measure of the input and output data set, then the following theorem also holds:
\begin{theorem}[Theorem 1.2, \cite{auffinger-tsne}] \label{thm:auffinger-tsne_1.2}
    Assume the same conditions as in theorem \ref{thm:auffinger-tsne_1.1}. Then there exists a measure $\mu^*$ and a sub-sequence $\{n_k\}$ such that $\mu_{n_k}$ converges weakly to $\mu^*$, and $\mu^* = \inf_{\mu \in \mathcal{P}_{\mu_X}} I_{\rho}(\mu)$. Moreover, $\mu^*$ has compact support.
\end{theorem}
The formulation of the theorems are slightly different from the original paper and some technical details are omitted due to the introductory nature of this chapter. The above two theorems send a strong message about the theoretical guarantee of t-SNE. If the input data is drawn independently from the same distribution, t-SNE is able to output a global minimum equilibrium measure (here measure means the distribution of the output data set) as we increase the sample size to infinity, with compact support. These are the first rigorous mathematical results about this algorithm after it has been used for so many years. And it leads to the next question, the question we are able to address in chapter 2.

For the output distributions, using the Gaussian is not necessary as the
successful execution of the algorithm does not rely on the specific properties
of Gaussian functions. The two theorems above further verify this observation.
This gives rise to the concept of kernels. For a pair of points $(x,y)$, a
kernel is a positive function of $\norm{x-y}$ that we can use to determine the
normalizing weights that produce the input and output distributions. For input,
the kernel is the Gaussian function and for the output, the kernel is the
function $f(x) = (1 + x^2)^{-1}$. It is natural to ask:

\textbf{``Is the student t-distribution not the only kernel that might give us the same theoretical guarantees? If no, then what is the class of output kernels that will give us the same results in \cite{auffinger-tsne}? Even more generally speaking, if we swap out both input and output kernels, what conditions do we need for the same theorems to be true?"}

These are the exact questions we are able to answer with rigorous proofs in
chapter 2. Besides that, people are already swapping out output kernels in
practice. Note that in the formula of KL-divergence, for the term
$p_{ij}\log(p_{ij}/q_{ij})$ reaches its minimum zero when $p_{ij} = q_{ij}$.
This means if we manually increase $p_{ij}$, the gradient descent process will
tend to increase $q_{ij}$ as well. This is achieved by putting the output
points $Y_i$ and $Y_j$ closer together, as the output kernel $f(x) = (1 +
    x^2)^{-1}$ is monotone decreasing. Therefore, in application people often
replace $p_{ij}$ with its multiple $kp_{ij}$ for some constant $k > 1$. This is
called the early exaggeration, one of the frequently used techniques. Using
early exaggeration will result in closer clusters, improving visualization
results.

Another known modification of the t-SNE algorithm is the heavy-tailed t-SNE.
Recall that the density function of the t-distribution is given by
\begin{align} \label{t_dist_density}
    f(x) = \left(1+\frac{x^2}{\alpha}\right)^{-\frac{\alpha+1}{2}},
\end{align}
where $\alpha$ is the degree of freedom. Decreasing $\alpha$ will reduce its decaying speed and make it more ``heavy-tailed". This will make $q_{ij}$ relatively larger and the output clusters will be compact. It is also shown in \cite{Kobak_2020} that heavy-tailed t-SNE tends to produce finer clusters, such as sub-clusters of the same digit in the MNIST data set.
\begin{figure}[ht]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=14cm]{heavytail_tsne.jpg}
    \caption{The running result of different SNE algorithms in \cite{Kobak_2020}, with (A) being the light-tail t-SNE, (B) the normal t-SNE and (C) the heavy-tailed t-SNE. Heavy tailed variants are producing tighter and finer clusters, while the light-tail version produces results similar to the original SNE. ($t$-distribution will converge to Gaussian if $\alpha \to \infty$.)}%
    \label{fig:heavytail_tsne}
\end{figure}
Both early exaggeration and heavy-tailed t-SNE can be seen as manipulations of the output kernel. t-SNE works effectively under the modification and produces promising results, besides different characteristics researchers hope to see. Besides exploring different conditions for the convergence to hold and the equilibrium measure to exist, it is also an interesting problem to see how exactly the output changes with the modification of the output kernel. Some insightful results can be found in \cite{weinkove2024flow}.

It is fascinating to see the rigorous mathematical theory behind widely used
algorithms like t-SNE, which are often used to produce qualitative rather than
quantitative results. Getting to the bottom of the convergence behavior will
provide valuable insights on the algorithm itself, refining the theoretical
landscape of the dimension reduction theory. We will resume our journey in
chapter 2.

\section{Markov Random Field and Probabilistic Graphical Models (PGM)} \label{intro_chap:sec:MRF}
\subsection{Dependency: An Unavoidable Problem} \label{intro_chap:sec:MRF:intro}
We have seen how the theory of dimension reduction has come a long way for the
purpose of addressing the challenges from high-dimensional data. Now we turn to
another, likely more fundamental problem that probability, statistics and data
science need to face: dependency. Though most of probability textbook starts
with the concept of independence and theorems assuming it, dependencies are
probably one of the oldest and most prevalent challenges we have had to tackle
ever since the concept of probability was born. How farmers might change their
planting plans based on weather; how gamblers leverage their bets based on the
cards in hand; how traders design their investment strategies based on the
stock market. All these decisions are based on dependencies and uncertainty.
Probabilistic dependency has been with us since the beginning of our time. Even
to this day, people might still care about the fate of Schrodinger's cat,
``condition on" the mysterious nature of quantum superposition.

The explosive advancement of computer science and modern industry not only
brings high-dimensional data, but also exponentially more complicated
dependencies that come with it. Let us take a look at several
examples\footnote{All images used in this section are under free or open
    copyright licenses such as CC BY-SA and CC BY. All images have been properly
    credited.} that demonstrate the difficulties and complexity of such problems.
The first instance is image segmentation and the famous paper written by Geman
and Geman (\cite{Geman1984}). With the introduction of digital imagery, we
receive a grid of many pixels, as well as a grid of intricate dependencies. The
color and intensity of any given pixel is highly likely to be similar to that
of its immediate neighbors. This is true for almost all real images that
include actual content. Pixels from the same object they depict tend to share
common characteristics, and that is how our eyes capture and process the world
we see through various coherence. If we treat all pixels as independent noises,
we will be getting static noise that does not convey any information.
Therefore, the authors treat the problem of image segmentation and denoising as
a statistical inference problem, with Markov random field being the main tool
they deployed to encode the information.

\begin{figure}[ht]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=8cm]{Image_segmentation.png}
    \caption{A simple example of image segmentation, by B Palac, from Wikimedia Commons. Different objects like the bus, the road and the buildings in the background can be detected and identified by algorithms. This technology is widely used in critical fields like self-driving cars.}%
    \label{fig:image_segmentation}
\end{figure}

This paper is one of the most famous and important paper in the field of
computer vision and pattern analysis. It utilizes a lot of important tools and
concepts like Bayesian inference, Gibbs sampling, and Markov random field, some
of which will be introduced more systematically in the next several sections.
The paper also draws inspiration from statistical physics and today the
intersection between statistical physics, math, and computer science has
developed into a even larger and more captivating field. It is easy to write a
couple of books just based on the keywords from this single paper so we will
not go into too much nuances. Rather, let us talk about some of the real-life
impact this discovery brought.

\begin{figure}[ht]
    \centering

Waymo uses a mixture of technologies, with computer vision and image segmentation being one of the key component of its smooth operation.}%
    \label{fig:waymo}
\end{figure}

One of the major application of image segmentation is self-driving cars. In
order to identify various objects on the road and navigate through
ever-changing traffic, self-driving cars need to have cameras and computer
vision technologies to detect and identify objects. This is the aspect that
cameras can be much more useful than radars (while radars are better handling
positional and distance information). In 2025, we already have commercialized
self-driving taxi services by Waymo in several major cities like San Francisco,
Los Angeles, and Phoenix, with some known for its notoriously difficult traffic
conditions. Not only doing business, Waymo is also active in related research
fields, and some of its published results still draw inspiration from Geman and
Geman in 1984 (\cite{zhu2023superpixeltransformersefficientsemantic}).

    \label{fig:more_image_segmentation}

Another well-known and important follow-up is the Graph Cuts, emerging in early
2000s (\cite{Boykov2001}, \cite{Kolmogorov2004}, \cite{Boykov2004}). This new
image segmentation algorithm was solid step up from Geman and Geman's work and
it profoundly influenced the world. Due to its massive improvement in
computational efficiency and overall perforamnce, it is widely used in many
areas like medical imaging. MRI and CT are able to identify organs, lesions and
tissues with the help of segmentation and it is safe to say these devices saved
countless lives. Graph Cuts also contributed to the development of stereo
vision, which allows the depth of images to be recovered by using two or more
cameras, similar to how our eyes work. The narratives and stories above can all
be traced back to 1984, where the dependencies problems were effectively
modelled and addressed. Vision is the most common sense we use to perceive the
world. It contains great obstacles just as it contains great values.

Another good example of dependency research is again, not surprisingly, about
genetic sequencing. One of the core reasons that genetic research is extremely
difficult is the dependencies between different genes. They do not work in
isolation. The expression level of one gene can activate or inhibit the
expression of others such as the lac Operon and the trp Operon in E. coli
(\cite{Naville2010}, \cite{JACOB1961318}). For humans a well known genetic
piece is the Estrogen Receptor (ER$\alpha$). ER$\alpha$ plays a vital role in
the development and function of many tissues, particularly in the female
reproduction system, but also in bone, brain and cardiovascular health.
ER$\alpha$ affects our body primarily by interacting and binding with other DNA
sequences, which ultimately leads to changes in cell behavior. Particularly,
ER$\alpha$ dysregulation is a key driver in the majority of breast cancers
(\cite{10.1158/0008-5472.CAN-03-3326}, \cite{doi:10.1152/physrev.00026.2006}).
Drugs that block estrogen binding or ER$\alpha$ activity are critical therapies
for breast cancer treatments.

\begin{figure}[ht]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=14cm]{Gene_regulatory_networks.jpg}
    \caption{An example of GRN, by Min Oh, Jaegyoon Ahn, and Youngmi Yoon, from Wikimedia Commons. Graph structure is crucial in visualizing complex relationships between genes and different types of cancers.}%
    \label{fig:gene_regulatory_networks}
\end{figure}

This is just one graph of the vast nest of dependencies in our genetic
sequence. Therefore scientists felt it imperative to use mathematical tools to
encode, analyze and understand the dependency relationship. Gene Regulatory
Networks (GRN) is one of such creations. GRNs use Bayesian Networks (we will
cover more details in section \ref{intro_chap:sec:MRF:sec:BN_MRF}.) to model
the relations between genetics and cellular function, development and disease.
By representing genes as nodes and regulatory influences as directed edges,
scientists can infer plausible probabilistic relationships between gene
expression levels, even in the presence of noise
(\cite{doi:10.1089/106652700750050961}, \cite{Sachs2005}). GRNs are often
better understood visually than through textual explanation. Please see figure
\ref{fig:gene_regulatory_networks} to see how five different types of cancers
are related to a large collection of genes via different types of regulatory
influences. Without the help of probability and Bayesian network, such
information is impossible to be extracted.

While GRNs map interactions between genes, Hidden Markov Models (HMM) aim to
decipher the functional grammar within a single sequence (\cite{Krogh1994HMM},
\cite{Durbin_Eddy_Krogh_Mitchison_1998}). It is another extremely important
tool in genetic research like GRN but with a different focus. One key concept
HMM has that is not present in GRN is called the hidden states. Generally
speaking, the observed gene sequence is just the tip of the iceberg. There are
so many actives and biochemical reactions that happen before a segment of gene
is formed and observed. To give an instance, for eukaryotic genes we have
concepts of exon and intron. Exons are the coding regions that are present in
the final mRNA transcript, while introns are the non-coding regions that get
removed during RNA splicing. One of the key functions of HMM is to identify the
hidden states, the status ``behind the scene", that connect an intron state to
a possible exon state, using a framework similar to the Markov chain, hence the
name. For example, if we have a hidden state with some particular intron, HMM
will find the transition probability of this state to other hidden states, and
ultimately, to different observed sequences.

\begin{figure}[ht]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=10cm]{A_profile_HMM_modelling_a_multiple_sequence_alignment.png}
    \caption{A illustration about HMM modeling multiple sequence alignment, by EMBL-EBI Train Online, from Wikimedia Commons.}%
    \label{fig:HMM_modeling}
\end{figure}

Contrary to GRNs, whose goal is often the network structure itself, HMMs are
used for classification and alignment, such as calculating the probability that
a given sequence was generated by a particular collections of hidden states. We
call this the emission probabilities. If we see a coding regions with a certain
sequence of nucleotide A, C, G, T or some types of amino acids, we are likely
able to predict the likelihood of obtaining a specific observable symbol. HMM
reveals and decodes the mechanism behind gene formation, allowing us to
understand the process of gene expression and regulation on a systematic level.

For both examples we just went through (image segmentation and genetic
sequencing), we can see the incredible values contained within dependencies,
because dependencies encode relations. It is relations that define the world we
live in, not isolated objects. All the applications in those examples benefit
from properly modelled dependencies. And all those dependencies are transcribed
in graph structures. Indeed, probabilistic graphical models are specifically
designed to address the dependencies problems, and there have been so many
successful advancements and applications of PGMs. As we venture deeper into the
finer structure of this universe, both on micro and macro scales, PGMs have
become even more important than ever before.

\begin{remark}
    Though PGMs are the specialized tool to solve dependency problems, we should also be aware that they are not the only tools. As vast as the theories and applications of PGMs have come to be, they are a small part of a much larger overarching theme, combinatorial optimization (CO). This term can refer to any problems that aim to find an optimal object from a finite set of objects (\cite{Schrijver2003}), often comes with a set of constraints as well. Probabilistic dependency is only one kind of constraints and PGMs are only one type of tools that record such constraints. The field of CO is extremely diverse and rich. We can start with basic examples like the traveling salesman problem all the way to the major unsolved problems such as P=NP. It is easy to go too deep into the rabbit hole. Therefore, it would be appropriate to acknowledge the wider picture while focusing on PGMs in this section.
\end{remark}
\subsection{PGM, a Short History} \label{intro_chap:sec:MRF:sec:PGM_history}
Both graph theory and probability have a long history. Though the foundation of
modern probability is widely regarded as the work by Kolmogorov, probability
theory, in general, can date back to the 16th century
(\cite{lightner1991brief}). Graph theory is often believed to be the brain
child of Euler (\cite{Euler1736}). The basic ingredients of PGM have existed
long before PGM itself was created. Therefore, the history of PGM can be
roughly divided into three parts: the early conceptual seeds (from probability
and graph theory), the emergence of mainstream models (Bayesian networks and
Markov random fields), and the unification/expansion era that came with the
advent of modern computer science.

Prior to the 1980s, there was not a definitive concept of PGM that was widely
acknowledged and used among the research community. However, the practices that
used graphs to represent probabilistic models started to emerge in the first
half of the 20th century. In 1925, Ernst Ising used the lattice graph in his
Ph. D thesis to represent the the magnetic dipole moments of atomic ``spins"
(\cite{Ising1925BeitragZT}). Later this idea was generalized by Lars Onsager in
1944 (\cite{PhysRev.65.117}) and then evolved into the famous Ising model we
know today in statistical physics. Formally speaking, let $\Lambda$ be the
lattice graph(site) and we use positive integers to denote its vertices. For
each vertex(site) $k$, there is a discrete variable $\sigma_k$ such that
$\sigma_k \in \{-1,1\}$, representing the site's spin. We call the collection
of all spins $\sigma := \{\sigma_k\}_{k \in \Lambda}$ the spin configuration.
For any two adjacent sites(vertices) $i,j \in \Lambda$, there is an interaction
$J_{ij}$. Finally, let $\mu$ be the magnet moment and $h_j$ be the external
magnetic field interacting with $j$. The Ising model is defined by the
following random function called the Hamiltonian:
\begin{align}
    H(\sigma) = -\sum_{<ij>} J_{ij} \sigma_i \sigma_j - \mu \sum_{j \in \Lambda} h_j \sigma_j,
\end{align}
where the first summation is over all pairs of vertices/sites $i,j$ that are adjacent on the lattice. Ising model is widely considered the first model in the study of spin glasses. The Hamiltonian effectively captures the interaction, thus dependencies between each spin via a graph structure. Exchanging the lattice graph with other types of graph will give us some of the other famous models, such as the Sherrington-Kirkpatrick model (complete graph) and Edwards-Anderson model (high-dimensional lattice). Though by definition spin glass models can be seen as PGMs, the studies about them do not have a strong relation with tools in graph theory. In fact, it is a drastically different field compared to the topic we are discussing in chapter 3. Rather, we mention Ising model to highlight the novel representation of mathematical objects that bring probability and graph theory together.

Another early example is path analysis introduced by Sewall Wright in 1921
(\cite{Wright1921CorrelationAndCausation}, \cite{Wright1934}). This method was
created to understand and quantify the relationships among a set of correlated
random variables, based on a hypothesized causal structure. Wright represented
the hypothesized causal relationship using path diagrams (essentially Directed
Acyclic Graphs or DAGs), where nodes represent variables and arrows represent
direct causal influences.

\begin{figure}[ht]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=13cm]{casualmap.png}
    \caption{An intuitive example of path diagrams with edge weights (contributing factors). The daily activities of a person depend on the transportation and food choices, which further depend on the weather, mood and how hungry the person is. Using the diagram, we can conduct analysis such as estimating the impact of weather on the person's daily activities.}%
    \label{fig:casualmap}
\end{figure}

With the help of directed graphs, complicated chain of influence can be
decomposed and quantitatively studied. The correlation/causation between two
variables are measured by the sum of all paths that connect them and the
contribution of a path is determined via the product of all its edge weight.
This simple but effective framework allows us to estimate the magnitude of
direct and indirect effects within the causal model.

\begin{figure}[ht]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=11cm]{wrightpathanalysis.png}
    \caption{A path analysis diagram from \cite{10.1093/genetics/6.2.111}. Wright used this diagram to visualize and analyze the relations between two mated individuals and their progeny.}%
    \label{fig:wrightpathanalysis}
\end{figure}

Wright himself was also a population geneticist and he used path analysis
extensively to study inheritance. Some of his famous work included the piebald
patterns of Guinea-Pigs and factor analysis for offspring phenotype
(\cite{Wright1920TheRI}, \cite{10.1093/genetics/6.2.111}). In fact, Wright had
already started using the concept of path analysis in 1918 before he formally
defined the term (\cite{10.1093/genetics/3.4.367}). It is safe to say the his
biological research inspired the creation of this important mathematical model,
yet another great victory for the interdisciplinary research between math and
biology!

It is also worth noting that the Wright's work has profoundly influenced the
field of PGM and computer science. Path analysis is considered a direct
precursor to modern Structural Equation Modeling (SEM). In 1960s and 1970s,
social scientist used path analysis and created a set of tools that are still
widely used today in social science, psychology and economics
(\cite{Duncan1975}, \cite{Bollen89}).

The inspiration of path analysis did not stop there. Fast-forwarding to the
1980s, the concept of PGM was widely recognized and used with the invention of
Bayesian networks and Markov random fields, the two pillars of the realm.
Continuing from Wright's work, Judea Pearl formalized Bayesian Networks (BN) in
1988 (\cite{10.5555/534975}). Similar to path analysis, BN uses nodes to
represent variables and arrows (directed edges) to represent direct dependence.
On top of that, BN accurately captures the conditional probability using the
chain decomposition. Let $X_1, X_2, \ldots, X_n$ be a collection of random
variables and $p(x_1,\dots, x_n)$ their joint density function. We have
\begin{align}
    p(x_1,\dots, x_n) = p(x_1)p(x_2|x_1)\cdots p(x_n|x_1,\dots,x_{n-1}).
\end{align}
BN uses the decomposition, but based on the graph structure. So compared to path analysis, the computation of weight in BN produces actual probability. This greatly increase the information density of the same framework. Using BNs, we are able to effectively navigate through complex dependency relationships and compute the probability of events from evidence.

BNs have been widely used in various fields. Besides biology and medical
research, BNs are also used in software engineering and finance. One of the
most well-known application of BNs is the email spam filter
(\cite{Sahami1998}). The early version of email spam filters had very simple
structures. It looked at the overall probability of an email being potential
spam and conditional probability when certain words are present. Intuitively it
worked a lot like how we detect scam calls in some way. When we pick up a phone
call, if the number is unknown, then we will likely raise our suspicion
(conditional scam probability goes up). Then if the caller starts to ask for
personal information or request money, then the conditional probability of the
call being a scam goes up even more. We then most likely hang up because there
is a Bayesian network-like process that just happens in our head. Today, email
spam filters have got much more sophisticated, with some including LLMs.
However, LLMs are expensive to run so traditional, BN-based email filters are
still the mainstream choices, keeping billions of people safe from spam emails
every day. For finance, the nature of BNs makes it the ideal tool to model
potential risks (\cite{BNRisk2003}, \cite{Gemela2001}). Risks and risk
assessment can be generalized as attempts to answer the ``what if" questions.
In our daily lives, such questions can be easy. ``What if I got a flat tire?"
``Prepare a tire repair kit in your trunk.'' Financial market and related risks
have much more intertwined underlying causes. Such needs directly overlap with
the specialty of BNs, making it the ideal tool for risk assessment and
management til this day.

Now let us turn our attention to Markov Random Field (MRF). While Bayesian
networks are very effective in representing direct dependencies and causal
relationships. There are other types of links lacking clear ``directionality".
As with the pixels of a picture we previously mentioned. It is clear that two
adjacent pixels in a picture are likely to be correlated because they can
depict the same object. However, it is impossible, or more accurately,
logically unsound to say that one pixel causes the other. There are a lot more
examples where the correlations are undirected or symmetric. In order to model
those probabilistic dependencies, we need a different PGM other than BN. That
is where MRF comes in.

The formalization of MRF is widely considered to be the work of Besag in 1974
(\cite{Besag1974}). In this famous paper, Besag also presented an alternate
proof for the famous Hammersley-Clifford theorem, showcasing the MRF as a
powerful tool in terms of modeling dependencies based on geographic proximity.
We will not go into the details of the proof, but it would be beneficial to
state the theorem and compare the notion of MRF with a traditional Markov
Chain.

Let $[n] = \{1,2,\dots,n\}$ and $\mathcal{X} = (X_t)_{t \in [n]}$ be a finite
dimension random vector with each $X_t$ taking values in a finite set
$\mathcal{S}_t = \{1,2,\dots,m_t\}$. Suppose $[n]$ is the set of vertices of a
graph. For each $t$, denote $\mathcal{N}_t$ the set of vertices $s$ such that
$s$ is adjacent to $t$ in the graph.

\begin{definition} \label{def:MRF}
    The process $(X_t)_{t \in [n]}$ is said to be a Markov random field if

    (i) for each tuple $x = (x_1,x_2,\dots,x_n) \in \prod_{i=1}^n \mathcal{S}_i$, the joint probability satisfies
    \begin{align}
        P(X_1 = x_1, X_2 = x_2, \dots, X_n = x_n) >0;
    \end{align}

    (ii) for each $t$ and $x$,
    \begin{align}
        \p \{X_t = x_t | X_s = x_s, s \neq t\} = \p \{X_t = x_t | X_s = x_s, s \in \mathcal{N}_t \}.
    \end{align}
\end{definition}

We can see that the definition of MRF resembles much of the discrete-time
Markov chain. Instead of the sequential Markov property, MRF has its own Markov
property induced by the graph topology. A subset of vertices $A \subset [n]$ is
said to be complete (or a clique) if each pair of vertices is connected with an
edge in the graph. We can use this notion to define a Gibbs distribution.
\begin{definition}
    Let $x = (x_1,x_2,\dots,x_n) \in \prod_{i=1}^n \mathcal{S}_i$ be an arbitrary tuple from the joint distribution, and $\mathcal{C}$ the collection of all complete vertex subsets. A probability distribution $Q$ is called a Gibbs distribution (or Gibbs random field) if it can be written in the form
    \begin{align}
        Q(x) = \prod_{A \in \mathcal{C}} V_A(x),
    \end{align}
    where $V_A(x)$ is a positive function that depends only on $x_i$'s with $i \in A$.
\end{definition}
It is clear that each process $(X_t)_{t \in [n]}$ has a corresponding Gibbs distribution based on the dependencies. Now we can state the powerful Hammersley–Clifford theorem:
\begin{theorem}[Hammersley–Clifford theorem]
    Let $Q$ be a probability distribution on $\prod_{i=1}^n \mathcal{S}_i$. Then $Q$ is a Gibbs distribution if and only if it is a Markov random field with respect to the same graph structure.
\end{theorem}
Besag's paper is a major cornerstone in statistics and fields dealing with spatial data. Before his work, defining a valid joint probability distribution over many interacting variables was often difficult. Besag showed how one could specify the model based on conditional probability of each variable given its neighbors (using the local Markov property). Such practice is much more intuitive and actionable. On the other hand, his paper did not stop at a theoretical level. Besag also proposed practical methods for statistical analysis using pseudo-likelihood, which uses conditional probabilities to approximate maximum likelihood. Such estimations used to be intractable due to computational difficulties. Bridging theory and application, this paper was a huge achievement in the field of statistical learning that inspired countless advancements after it, such as the work by Geman and Geman (\cite{Geman1984}) we mentioned in section \ref{intro_chap:sec:MRF:intro}.

\begin{figure}[ht]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=12cm]{ICMBesag.png}
    \caption{Besag used MRF to develop the Iterated Condition Modes (ICM) algorithm that is able to restore dirty images. The figure is from his work in 1986 \cite{Besag1986}. As we can see, ICM performs much better than the old maximum-likelihood classifier.}%
    \label{fig:ICMBesag}
\end{figure}

The introduction of BN and MRF made researchers realize the power of PGMs.
Also, graph theory and probability happen to be two of the mathematical
sub-fields that are most related to computer science. People started to give
PGMs much more focus, aiming to unify the models and theories so that a more
comprehensive framework could serve to tackle the dependency problems emerging
from complex systems and machine learning.

Factor graph was created for this very purpose in 2001
(\cite{Kschischang2001}). As the name suggests, factor graphs provide a general
representation that explicitly shows the factorization of a global function
into local factors. The joint probability we discussed above for MRF is a good
example. They connect widely used models like MRF and BN, then unify the
inference algorithms like belief propagation. The lineage from both MRF and BN
is very clear.

\begin{figure}[ht]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=8cm]{beliefprop.png}
    \caption{The diagram illustrating belief propagation in \cite{Kschischang2001}. Belief propagation is a powerful model built upon BN and MRF that are broadly used in information theory and other related fields.}%
    \label{fig:beliefprop}
\end{figure}

There were also other new ideas rising from the fertile soil of PGMs, such as
Junction Trees, Variational Inference and MCMC (\cite{Jordan1999},
\cite{Gelfand01061990}). On the larger scale, the development of machine
learning was also gradually shifting the fashion of the field. Instead of
relying on expert knowledge, people began to use ML algorithms to learn the
unknown parameters or the graph structures from complex data, building the
navigation chart amidst the black sea of chaos. The effectiveness of PGMs,
combined with the strong generative power of ML, expand the potentials of PGMs
even further. It is exciting to see what this field will bring us for the rest
of this century.
\subsection{Bayesian Networks and Markov Random Fields} \label{intro_chap:sec:MRF:sec:BN_MRF}
As we mentioned in section \ref{intro_chap:sec:MRF:sec:PGM_history}, Bayesian
networks and Markov random fields represent the two main types of PGMs,
reflecting to choice of directed or undirected graph. In chapter 3, we will
provide a self-contained walkthrough on how machine learning algorithms can be
used to learn the graph structure. Towards that end, in this chapter, we will
dedicate some space to introduce the two main PGMs themselves, as well as the
\lovasz Local Lemma. This way we will have a better understanding of the
background and how chapter 3 weaves different topics together to create a new
algorithm.

Let us start with Bayesian network. Let $p(x_1,\dots,x_n)$ be any probability
density function. Recall that we can decompose $p$ using a chain of condition
probability:
\begin{align}
    p(x_1,\dots,x_n) = p(x_1)p(x_2|x_1)\cdots p(x_n|x_1,\dots,x_{n-1}).
\end{align}

Such decomposition is often computationally intractable. For BN, we modify such
decomposition that each $x_i$ only depends on a small subset of ancestor
variables $x_{A_i}$. For example, whether one takes the walk or bus depends on
tomorrow's weather, and the weather depends a series of complicated natural
factors. It would be an overkill if we model the choices of transportation on
all these factors. Instead, we just let the weather be the only ancestor
variable of the transportation choices and weather itself depends on the
natural factors such as temperature, wind direction, etc. Bearing the intuition
in mind, we can now give the formal definition of BN.

\begin{definition}
    A Bayesian network is a directed acyclic graph (DAG) $G = (V:=[n],E)$ such that:

    (i) each node $i$ represents a random variable $X_i$;

    (ii) for each $X_i$, there is a corresponding conditional probability distribution $p(x_i|x_{A_i})$ where $A_i$ consists of all the immediate ancestors of $i$ in the graph $G$.

    Conversely, we say a probability distribution $p$ factorizes over a DAG $G$ if
    it can be decomposed into a product of factors induced by $G$.
\end{definition}

Let us take a look at a simple example from \cite{koller2009probabilistic}.

\begin{figure}[ht]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=8cm]{BNstudent.png}
    \caption{Figure 3.3 from \cite{koller2009probabilistic}, a simple BN that models the letter graph and SAT scores of students.}%
    \label{fig:BNstudent}
\end{figure}

Denote difficulties, grade, intelligence, SAT, and Letter by their first letter
$d,i,g,s,l$ respectively. We can decompose the joint probability $p(d,i,g,s,l)$
in figure \ref{fig:BNstudent} starting from end nodes $s$ and $l$ and moving
upwards through the ancestry:
\begin{align}
    p(d,i,g,s,l) = p(s|i) \cdot p(l|g) p(g |i,d)p(i)p(d).
\end{align}
The reason that the example is well constructed is that not only it provides an easy-to-follow computation example, but also provides the three main patterns of conditional dependence in BN, based on whether the ancestor or intermediate node is observed or not. For three random variables $X,Y,Z$, we write $X \bot Y |Z$ if $X$ and $Y$ are independent condition on $Z$. We also use arrows to denote the local graph structure. For example, $X \to Y$ means there is a directed edge from $X$ to $Y$. The three common patterns are as follows.

\begin{figure}[ht]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=8cm]{BNpatterns.png}
    \caption{Figure 3.5 from \cite{koller2009probabilistic} that shows common connecting patterns in BN.}%
    \label{fig:BNpatterns}
\end{figure}

(i) Common cause, corresponding to (c) in the figure \ref{fig:BNpatterns} above. If $Z$ is unobserved, then obviously $X$ and $Y$ are not independent of each other because they share a common part of their randomness. However, if we know $Z$, then $X \bot Y |Z$ as the shared common part is determined and the rest of randomness is independent. To see that mathematically, first notice that we can write the joint probability as
\begin{align}
    p(X,Y,Z) = p(X|Z)p(Y|Z)p(Z).
\end{align}
For any $x,y,z$, $z$ fixed, we have
\begin{align}
      & \p(X=x,Y=y|Z=z) = \p(X=x|Z=z)\p(Y=y|Z=z)\p(Z=z|Z=z) \nonumber \\
    = & \p(X=x|Z=z)\p(Y=y|Z=z)
\end{align}
by the definition of conditional independence.

(ii) Common effect, which is (d) in the figure \ref{fig:BNpatterns}. If $Z$ is unobserved, then $X$ and $Y$ are independent of each other. However, if we know $Z$, then $X$ and $Y$ are not independent of each other. The mathematical proof follows similarly as the example in (i) above. Moreover, it makes intuitive sense if we look at figure \ref{fig:BNstudent} for the relationship among difficulty, intelligence and grade. Of course, the difficulty of the exams and the intelligence of students are independent as they are the results from different entities (exam makers and students). However, if we know the grade of a student, say a very high grade for a hard exam, that will change the joint probability. Performing well on a hard exam is more likely to come from a smart student. Difficulty and intelligence are no longer independent in this case.

(iii) Evidential and causal effects, representing (a) and (b) in figure \ref{fig:BNpatterns}. Clearly for unobserved $Z$ we have $X$ and $Y$ are not independent. However, if the intermediate node $Z$ is observed, then $X \bot Y |Z$. This means that everything it takes to determine the final state is provided, so the root in the network no longer plays a role in the outcome of its children. This is also clear in figure \ref{fig:BNstudent}. If the exam result is released, then the letter grade of a student will depend solely on the score, not on the smartness of the student.

The three patterns can be used to define another important concept called
d-separation. As we just see, dependency can be passed through paths on the
graph via a series of 3-node patterns given observed data. If there are no such
way to connect two clusters of nodes, then they are essentially conditionally
independent, revealing insights of the data we are trying to analyze.

\begin{definition}[d-separation]
    Let $\mathcal{X}$, $\mathcal{Y}$ and $\mathcal{O}$ be three sets of nodes in a Bayesian network $G$. We say that $\mathcal{X}$ and $\mathcal{Y}$ are d-separated given $\mathcal{O}$ (The letter $\mathcal{O}$ stands for observed) if $\mathcal{X}$ and $\mathcal{Y}$ are not connected by an active path. An undirected path in $G$ (meaning one can go against the arrows) is called active given observed nodes $\mathcal{O}$ if for every sequence of consecutive three nodes $X,Y,Z$ on the path satisfies one of the following conditions:

    (i) $X \leftarrow Y \leftarrow Z$, $Y \notin \mathcal{O}$;

    (ii) $X \to Y \to Z$, $Y \notin \mathcal{O}$;

    (iii) $X \to Y \leftarrow Z$, $Y \in \mathcal{O}$.

    (iv) $X \leftarrow Y \to Z$, $Y \notin \mathcal{O}$.
\end{definition}

\begin{figure}[ht]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=13cm]{dsep.png}
    \caption{A pair BNs illustrating the concept of d-separation.}%
    \label{fig:dsep}
\end{figure}

In figure \ref{fig:dsep}, we mark the observed nodes with a darker gray color.
It is easy to check that node $A$ and $F$ are d-separated given $B$ and $C$ on
the left, while $B$ and $C$ are not d-separated given $A$ and $F$ on the right
($B \to D \to F \to E \to C$ is an active path). We then proceed to define
\begin{align}
    \mathcal{I}(G) = \{(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y},\mathcal{Z}) | \text{$\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y}$ are d-separated by $\mathcal{Z}$}\}.
\end{align}
This definition is where the power of graph theory starts to manifest. Recall that graph theory studies quantities about graphs that remain unchanged under various transformation and mapping, such as coloring and characteristic polynomial. The quantity $\mathcal{I}(G)$ is also one of such quantities tied to Bayesian network that helps identify different Bayesian networks with similar underlying structures. If we define two BNs $G$ and $G'$ as I-equivalent if $\mathcal{I}(G) = \mathcal{I}(G')$, then the following theorem holds

\begin{theorem}[Theorem 3.9, \cite{koller2009probabilistic}]
    Two Bayesian networks $G$ and $G'$ are I-equivalent if and only if there exists a sequence of network
    \begin{align}
        G = G_1, \dots, G_k = G'
    \end{align}
    such that the only difference between $G_i$ and $G_{i+1}$, $1 \leq i \leq k=1$ is a single reversal of a covered edge.
\end{theorem}

This theorem, combined with an algorithm that can find $\mathcal{I}(G)$ and
machine learning algorithms that can learn the graph structure, provides a
capable framework for studying highly complex objects with lots of intricate
dependencies. There are many other fascinating facts about BN but we should
stop here to prevent ourselves from deviating too far. Now we will turn our
attention to MRF.

Unlike BNs, the definition of an MRF does not derive strictly from
decomposition of conditional probability. Rather, it assigns a factor(weight)
to each clique(complete subgraph) in the graph.

\begin{figure}[ht]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=11cm]{MRFexample.png}
    \caption{Two MRFs with different edges (and thus cliques) from figure 4.4, \cite{koller2009probabilistic}.}%
    \label{fig:MRFexample}
\end{figure}

Take figure \ref{fig:MRFexample} for instance, in the left graph, there are
four complete subgraphs $(A,B)$, $(B,C)$, $(C,D)$ and $(A,D)$. For any value
assignments $(a,b,c,d)$ of the random variables $(A,B,C,D)$, we can write the
factor of the value assignment as
\begin{align}
    \tilde{p}(a,b,c,d) = \phi(a,b)\phi(b,c)\phi(c,d)\phi(a,d),
\end{align}
where $\phi$ can be any positive functions defined by the user. Normalizing the factor will give us the joint probability distribution of the MRF:
\begin{align}
    p(a,b,c,d) = \frac{1}{Z}\tilde{p}(a,b,c,d),
\end{align}
and
\begin{align}
    Z = \sum_{(a,b,c,d)} \tilde{p}(a,b,c,d)
\end{align}
is called the partition function. We have already defined MRF in definition \ref{def:MRF}. For the graph on the right, there are only two complete subgraphs, consisting of $A,B,D$ and $B,C,D$. Then the factor score has the form
\begin{align}
    \tilde{p}(a,b,c,d) = \phi(a,b,d)\phi(b,c,d).
\end{align}
Now we can give an alternative and equivalent definition based on the graph structure:
\begin{definition}[Alternative Definition of MRF]
    Let $X_1,\dots,X_n$ be random variables. A Markov Random Field (MRF) is the joint probability distribution of $(X_i)_{i \in [n]}$ over an undirected graph $G=(V:=[n],E)$ with each vertex $i$ representing the random variable $X_i$. In addition, the joint probability $p$ has the form
    \begin{align}
        p(x_1,\dots,x_n) = \frac{1}{Z} \prod_{C \in \mathcal{C}} \phi_C(x_C),
    \end{align}
    where $\mathcal{C}$ is the collection of all cliques(complete subgraphs) in $G$, each factor $\phi_C$ is a non-negative function, and
    \begin{align}
        Z = \sum_{x_1,\dots,x_n} \prod_{C \in \mathcal{C}} \phi_C(x_C)
    \end{align}
    is the normalizing partition function.
\end{definition}

\begin{figure}[ht]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=13cm]{MRFindep.png}
    \caption{An MRF with two indicated independent clusters. The nodes with darker grey in the middle are observed, causing the clusters on the left and right to be independent.}%
    \label{fig:MRFindep}
\end{figure}

While both are powerful, MRFs and BNs excel at modeling different types of
dependencies. Although neither is strictly more general, any BN distribution
can be represented by an MRF through a process called moralization. This
involves adding an edge between any pair of parents that share a common child
(`marrying parents') and then making all edges undirected. Furthermore, the
concept of conditional independence is defined differently: while BNs use
d-separation via active paths, two sets of nodes in an MRF are conditionally
independent given a third set if that third set separates them in the graph.
The idea follows similarly as the evidential and causal effects in BN: a path
of nodes connecting two clusters is the shared part of their randomness. If the
path is fully observed, then the rest of randomness is conditionally
independent. In particular, if all neighbors of a node are observed, then this
node is independent from the rest of the network.

Upon this point, we can see that MRF has several advantages over BN. First, MRF
is more versatile. We can apply MRF to a broader range of problems. Any BNs can
be converted to MRF but not vice versa, and especially when the dependencies of
the model are naturally symmetric. This gives us the second advantage, that
MRFs are able to describe certain dependency structures that cannot be captured
by BNs like $k$-CNF formulas.

However, there are reasons that we cannot just use MRF everywhere. The primary
drawback of MRF is the computation of $Z$. To compute the partition function
$Z$, one might need to sum over an exponential number of possible value
assignments. This summation is generally NP-hard. Moreover, since every BN is
required to be acyclic and directed, it is very easy to generate data using the
hierarchy of dependencies relations. Since MRF, in contrast, is undirected and
thus has no ``starting points" or ancestors, it is often much harder to
generate data from a MRF.

Since our work in chapter 3 mainly deals with intractable computations, we will
dedicate some space to discuss sampling and \lovasz Local Lemma in the next
section. We also encourage the reader to check out the textbook by Koller and
Friedman \cite{koller2009probabilistic} for a more comprehensive introduction
to PGMs, which is considered the standard textbook on the subject.

\subsection{The Necessity of Sampling and \lovasz Local Lemma} \label{intro_chap:sec:MRF:sec:sampling}
In the previous section, we mentioned that computing the partition function $Z$
in an MRF directly is likely to be computationally intractable. Therefore,
sampling algorithms are usually required so that we can avoid computations that
scale exponentially. This is a clever and natural idea and approach, but it
introduces a new problem.

As the theme throughout this section, dependency is everywhere in the data we
are trying to analyze. It often comes in the form of constraints, or a series
of ``bad events". For the machine learning algorithm to effectively learn the
PGM model, we need to make sure the samples we generate are from the actual
conditional distributions from the data. Therefore when we are trying to
sampling with constraints, it is important for us to know the (approximate)
answers of the following two questions:

(i) Given a set of constraints, do the samples satisfying all conditions exist?

(ii) If yes, how do we sample from the distribution effectively without spending exponential time or stuck in an finite loop. (Meeting one constraint will fail another. Sometimes constant adjustment without proper strategy will indefinitely prolong the sampling process.)

Though there is no one-size-fits-all answer to the two questions, there are
some general principles that can be applied to a wide range of problems. One of
the most famous and widely used principle is \lovasz Local Lemma (LLL),
discovered and proved by Erd\"os and \lovasz in 1975.

\begin{lemma}[\cite{LLL1975}] \label{lem:LLL1975}
    Let $A_1,\dots,A_k$ be a sequence of events such that each event occurs with probability at most $p$ and such that each event if independent of all the other events except for at most $d$ of them. Assume
    \begin{align}
        4pd \leq 1.
    \end{align}
    Then there is a nonzero probability that none of the events occurs.
\end{lemma}

Shearer improved the results in 1985 and the updated lemma becomes the \lovasz
Local Lemma(LLL) we know today:
\begin{lemma}[\cite{LLL1985}] \label{lem:LLL1985}
    Assuming the same conditions as lemma \ref{lem:LLL1975}, if we have $p \leq \frac{1}{2d}$. Assume
    \begin{align}
        p < & \frac{(d-1)^{(d-1)}}{d^d}, \quad d>1 \nonumber \\
        p < & \frac{1}{2}, \quad d=1.
    \end{align}
    Then there is a nonzero probability that none of the events occurs.
\end{lemma}

Both lemma \ref{lem:LLL1975} and \ref{lem:LLL1985} answered the first existence
problem. However, there is a lot of room for improvement. All the bad events
have a common upper bound $p$, which reduces the applicability of the lemmas.
Spencer in 1977 proved the asymmetric version of the lemma. To state this
result, we need to the concept of dependency graphs, which are extensively used
in chapter 3. To put in simple terms, a dependency graph is a graph with nodes
representing a set of events such that mutually independent events are not
adjacent, otherwise connected via an edge. With that being said, we have the
following statement.

\begin{lemma}[\cite{SPENCER197769}, Asymmetric Lovász local lemma]
    Let $\mathcal{A} = \{A_1, \dots, A_n\}$ be a finite set of events in the probability space $\Omega$. For $A \in \mathcal{A}$, let $\Gamma(A)$ be the neighbors of $A$ in the dependency graph. Assume there exists a map $f: \mathcal{A} \to [0,1)$ such that for every $A \in \mathcal{A}$,
    \begin{align}
        \p(A) \leq f(A) \prod_{B \in \Gamma(A)} (1-f(B)).
    \end{align}
    Then there exists a nonzero probability that none of the events in $\mathcal{A}$ occurs. Additionally
    \begin{align}
        \p\left(\bigcap_{i=1}^n \bar{A_i} \right) \geq \prod_{A \in \mathcal{A}} (1-f(A)).
    \end{align}
\end{lemma}

As we can see, the asymmetric LLL has a stronger result, even with a positive
lower bound. However, the improved existence does not lead to a actionable
algorithm that helps us sample from the distribution. All proofs of the three
lemmas above are non-constructive. In order to put this powerful mathematical
fact into practice, there has been many attempts seeking for constructive
proofs or better, an algorithmic version of LLL. To name a few, Beck in 1991
gave an algorithmic proof of LLL under polynomial time, one of the early
milestones that turned LLL into an effective sampling technique. The most
well-known result is probably by Moser and Tardos
(\cite{moser2009constructiveproofgenerallovasz}). Because of their improvement
and novelty, Robin Moser and Gábor Tardos received the Gödel Prize in 2020.

Thus far, we have introduced most background knowledge related to our work in
chapter 3. We started with the importance of the overarching dependency
problem, then proceeded to give a brief history of the tools we use. Finally,
we introduced LLL, which we later incorporate into a neural solver with
parallelism capability. Starting from graph theory and probability, the
intersection between various fields of mathematics and computer science never
ceases to amaze us with its beauty and potential.

\section{Factor Analysis and Variational Inference} \label{intro_chap:sec:FA_VI}
\subsection{Combining DR and PGM}
In previous sections \ref{intro_chap:sec:tsne} and \ref{intro_chap:sec:MRF}, we
have discussed the importance and difficulties of problems in probability and
data science that arise from high dimensionality and dependencies. To solve
these two problems, scientists created dimension reduction and probabilistic
graphic models to tackle them respectively. For our work in chapter 4, the
algorithm we develop involves both dimension reduction and probabilistic
graphic models. The framework we invented is called Variational inference
Factor Analysis (VarFA). It is self-explanatory that this toolkit combines
variational inference with factor analysis.

From the dimension reduction perspective, the variational inference part draws
inspiration of classical Variational AutoEncoder (VAE), which utilizes the
so-called ``latent dimensions" to generate output similar to the input data
set. To understand latent dimensions, let us take a look at some real-life
examples, such as modeling in games. For large games (such as games with vast
open worlds) it is impossible to create models for every single NPC, building
and tree, etc. One could use the same object repetitively, but seeing two
identical people on the street will definitely break immersion. Instead, game
developers want to generate a set of objects that are similar to the in-game
object they have already created, but different. If we see input image $X$ as
high-dimensional data with pixel information, then we want to find a
probabilistic algorithm that can compute the probability $p(X)$, where $p$
refers to the probability of how likely this collection of pixel can be seen as
similar objects created manually by developers. If $p$ is high, we take the
image and a new in-game object is generated.

\begin{figure}[ht]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=10cm]{VAEMNIST.png}
    \caption{The original handwritten digits from MNIST (top row) and VAE-generated digits (bottom row), from \cite{VAE2023}. Though some outputs are not entirely natural, all are recognizable and there is no mixing of two different digits.}
    \label{fig:VAEMNIST}
\end{figure}

But the task is not as easy as it seems. If we use brutal force approaches, we
can end up with an NPC with body of a human and head of a bus, if the pool of
hand-drawn objects contain both humans and buses. This will create a lot of
trouble. However, if the model is designed to assign a category to each of the
objects, such as human, vehicles, plants, etc, then it will know not to mix
things up when generating objects. The vector (human, vehicles, plants,
$\dots$) is the latent dimension we are talking about. In practice, latent
dimensions usually do not have meaningful labels, and they do not need to. Just
as t-SNE, the latent dimensions are used to represent different types of data
samples and make sure the output is consistent with the
categorization/representing clusters.

As for PGM viewpoint, the architecture of VarFA can be represented as a neural
network (directed graph), where each layers of input determines the
values/distributions of the next layers. The training process of the model is
essentially learning the parameters on the graph. This firmly places VarFA in
the PGM family. Indeed, as we can see in chapter 4, we model the
performance/grades of students based on latent skill levels. How well a student
perform on a specific test question can be a combination of related skills.
VarFA establishes the dependency structures between competence and exam scores,
not just predicting them.

borrow from VAE. With all the topics covered, the readers should have a
sufficient context to understand the motivation and contribution of our work in

\subsection{Lineage of Factor Analysis}
We have introduced the brief history of dimension reduction in section
\ref{intro_chap:sec:tsne:DR}, including a brief reference to VAE. In fact,
Factor Analysis (FA) is also a dimension reduction technique, but not in the
same fashion as the other algorithms we introduced earlier. Therefore, it would
be beneficial to give a short rundown of this approach before we move onto the
next section.

FA was widely considered to be the brain child of Charles Spearman
(\cite{Spearman_1904}), the famous statistician and psychologist known for the
famous ``Spearman Correlation''. In his famous paper, he conducted a series of
experiments on the performance of students in different school subjects and
found that although a lot of the subjects were seemingly unrelated, the
students' performance was consistent across the board. This led him to think
that there was one single underlying factor that determines the competence of
students, or colloquially speaking, ``smart kids can learn any subject quickly
and easily''. What he proposed was the so-called single-factor model,
associating students' performance with a single variable called ``general
mental ability''. Coincidentally, VarFA also focuses its application in
education and the modelling of students' performance. It is no doubt that
education is more than vital to the entire human society, and that is precisely
why we continue to go back to such topic, more so in the era of machine
learning and AI.

After Spearman's idea, various researchers tried to expand the model to
naturally include the cases where multiple factors are required to model and
predict the data. One of the most important cornerstones in this field is the
work by Thurstone (\cite{thurstone1947multiple}). This book includes various
important works starting from the 1930s. The discoveries in this period laid
the foundation of the FA framework we are familiar with today.

In particular, assume there are $p$ rounds of observations for $n$ individuals.
FA assumes that the collective behavior of the individuals can be explained by
$k$ common factors, and $k < p$. Mathematically speaking,
\begin{align} \label{eq:FA}
    x_{i,m} - \mu_i = \sum_{j=1}^k l_{i,j}f_{j,m} + \epsilon_{i,m}.
\end{align}
Here, $x_{i,m}$ is the $i$-th observation of the $m$-th individual, $\mu_i$ is the mean of the $i$-th observation, $l_{i,j}$ is the loading of the $j$-th factor on the $i$-th observation, $f_{j,m}$ is the $j$-th factor score for the $m$-th individual and $\epsilon_{i,m}$ is the error term, usually with zero mean and finite variance. This definition is the formulation of basically one sentence:

\textbf{For the samples we are investigating, the observations are linear combinations of the common factors plus some noise.}

And it is easy to see that \eqref{eq:FA} can be rewritten in the form of matrix
multiplication:
\begin{align}
    X - M = LF + \epsilon.
\end{align}

More details about factor analysis will be given in chapter 4. In this
introductory chapter, it is more important to address te unaddressed statement
at the beginning of this section: why is factor analysis different from other
dimension reduction techniques, though it seems very basic, only using linear
combinations and independent noise? The answer lies not in the mathematical
formulation, but rather the philosophy of usage. \textbf{FA is the quantitative
    catalyst of statistical intuition.}

To explain this answer, let us point out that FA can be divided into two
categories: Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA) and Confirmatory Factor Analysis
(CFA). EFA is created in 1930s and largely contributed by Thurstone
(\cite{thurstone1947multiple}). In EFA, the user does not specify the structure
of observations and is required to determine the number of factors. Afterwards,
the user needs to choose a rotation method to make the factors more
interpretable. Unlike PCA, EFA's goal is to help researchers interpret the
latent factors, whereas PCA stops after giving the data points in lower
dimensions. EFA aims to generate intuitions.Though closely related to PCA, the
mind set behind EFA was already different even in early 20th century.

This unique philosophy of EFA was extended further when CFA was created in
1960s by Jöreskog (\cite{Jöreskog_1967}, \cite{Jöreskog_1969}). The user now
needs to specify the structure of the model, from the number of factors and the
loadings for each of them. CFA was created to provide a statistically sound
procedure to verify what might be considered ``a good guess from experienced
researchers''. To use this model, researchers are required to think carefully
about the underlying structure of their measurements, and provide a plausible
model to evaluate those thoughts. CFA tests intuition.

The DR techniques we introduced in section \ref{intro_chap:sec:tsne} are mostly
automatic, input the data and the algorithm will do the rest for you.
Meanwhile, FA values human input and strives to make the results explainable.
Though a lot of advancement in today's AI comes with the caveat of ``black
box", FA's lineage was well preserved and inspired a lot of subsequent
discoveries in the field of Explainable Artificial Intelligence (XAI)
(\cite{doshivelez2017}, \cite{miller2018}). It is also the reason why we choose
FA to combine with the generative power of VAE. Our work in chapter 4 is not a
mere mix and swap of models, but rather a concrete practice of new mindsets
towards the goal of fostering human learning with machine learning.

\subsection{The Modern Toolkit: Variational Autoencoders} \label{intro_chap:sec:VarFA:sec:VAE}
Apart from FA, VAE is another core piece of the puzzle in our work about VarFA.
We brought up VAE in section \ref{intro_chap:sec:tsne:DR} briefly and now it is
a good time to formally introduce it. Similar to algorithms like t-SNE, VAE
uses the so-called latent dimensions that are often not interpretable. However,
unlike t-SNE, VAE is a generative model, meaning instead of visualization, it
is to generate new data points that follow the same distribution as the input
data. Invented by Welling and Kingma in 2013 (\cite{kingma2013auto}), not long
after CNN was coined. VAE is an important milestone in the field of artificial
intelligence because the capability of generating content based on training
materials back then was truly revolutionary.

That being said, VAE is not the silver bullet, far from it. As we will see very
soon in the deduction of the method, VAE relies on a lot of bold assumptions
and approximations, and offers little room for tuning. Unlike t-SNE and MRF,
VAE is not used in chapter 4, but extremely important in the overall
literature. Therefore we will give a more detailed walkthrough of this amazing
algorithm, focusing on the underlying assumptions and more explicit
mathematical details. (There are many other more standard materials on VAE,
such as the famous tutorial by Carl Doersch
\cite{doersch2021tutorialvariationalautoencoders}.)

Given a data point $\vx$ in high dimension, VAE assumes that $\vx$ is
determined by a fewer number of latent variables, denoted as a lower
dimensional vector $\vz$. Using conditional probability decomposition, we can
write
\begin{align}
    p(\vx|\vz) = \int p(\vx|\vz)p(\vz)d\vz \equiv \int p_{\theta}(\vx|\vz)p(\vz)d\vz,
\end{align}
where $\theta$ is the hyperparameter VAE is trying to learn. The exact formulation is given in \eqref{eq:pzbernoulli} and \eqref{eq:pzcontinuous}. The true bold claim here is not that data itself depends on latent variables, but the assumption that $p(z)$ is (often) a multi-dimensional random variable with i.i.d., standard normal entries. Bold as it might be, the assumption itself is in fact pretty well-grounded. For instance, your performance at a certain sport is determined by a series of factors such as your endurance and strength. It would be normal that both your strength and endurance follow a Gaussian-like distribution. Your endurance might fluctuate around your ``average'' level and performing significantly above or below your average is getting exponentially rare. Since all Gaussian distributions are the result of a simple affine transformation of the standard normal distribution, we might as well assume that the latent variables are i.i.d. standard normal from the very beginning. Furthermore, $p_{\theta}$ is also mostly set to be a Gaussian distribution if the target data is continuous following the same logic.

After giving $p(z)$ a distribution, determining the parameters for
$p_{\theta}(x|z)$ directly is not feasible. That is the primal motivation for
the encoder-decoder architecture. By Bayes formula, we can write
\begin{align}
    p_{\theta}(\vz|\vx) = \frac{p_{\theta}(\vx|\vz) p(\vz)}{p(\vx)} = \frac{p_{\theta}(\vx|\vz) p(\vz)}{\int p_{\theta}(\vx|\vz') p(\vz') d\vz'}.
\end{align}
This means we want to learn the relationship between the input data and the latent factors first before we start generating data. This way we can use the learned hyperparameter $\theta$ and samples from $p(\vz)$ to generate new data points. This leads to the next problem: $p(\vx)$ is also intractable. Therefore, we need another parametrized Gaussian distribution $q_{\phi}(\vz|\vx)$ to approximate $p_{\theta}(\vz|\vx)$. This is the second bold assumption, that the conditional distribution of latent variables given observed data is (almost) Gaussian. To give an intuitive example, suppose we see a student performing exceptionally well in a math exam, we might take a high guess at the student's math skill (mean), or the related latent variable. If the student is consistent, then we might decrease the variance. Formally speaking, $q_{\phi}(\vz|\vx)$ takes the following form:
\begin{align}
    q_{\phi}(\vz|\vx) = \Ndist(\vz; \vmu_{\phi}(\vx), \text{diag}(\vsigma^2_{\phi}(\vx))).
\end{align}
Here, $\vmu_{\phi}(\vx)$ is the mean vector. $\vsigma^2_{\phi}(\vx)$ is the variance vector (diagonal elements of the covariance matrix).
The encoder network takes $\vx$ as input and outputs the parameters $\vmu_{\phi}(\vx)$ and $\vsigma^2_{\phi}(\vx)$ (or often $\log \vsigma^2_{\phi}(\vx)$ for numerical stability). The explicit formula for the probability density function (PDF) of $q_{\phi}(\vz|\vx)$, assuming a $N$-dimensional latent space, is:
\begin{align}
    q_{\phi}(\vz|\vx) = \frac{1}{(2\pi)^{N/2} |\mSigma_{\phi}(\vx)|^{1/2}} \exp\left(-\frac{1}{2} (\vz - \vmu_{\phi}(\vx))^T \mSigma_{\phi}(\vx)^{-1} (\vz - \vmu_{\phi}(\vx))\right)
\end{align}
where $|\mSigma_{\phi}(\vx)|$ is the determinant of the covariance matrix $\mSigma_{\phi}(\vx)$, which simplifies to $\prod_{j=1}^{N} \sigma_{j, \phi}^2(\vx)$.

Let us take a pause here and recall that we make two bold assumptions during
the setup of the encoder-decoder framework. We assume that the latent variables
are i.i.d. standard normal random variables, and the conditional distribution
of latent variables given observed data is also Gaussian. Without these two
assumptions, VAE will not be computationally possible. So qualitatively
speaking, we can say VAE roots itself in one core belief:

\textbf{The data we are working on is intrinsically Gaussian.}

Now let us go back to the mathematical details. We call $p_{\theta}(\vz|\vx)$
the true posterior distribution, the distribution ``post the observation'' and
$p(z)$ the prior, the distribution ``prior to the observation''. We measure the
distance between our approximation $q_{\phi}(\vz|\vx)$ and
$p_{\theta}(\vz|\vx)$ using KL-divergence. By definition
\ref{def:KL_divergence}, we compute
\begin{align}
    \KL{q_{\phi}(\vz|\vx)}{p_{\theta}(\vz|\vx)} & = \int q_{\phi}(\vz|\vx) \log \frac{q_{\phi}(\vz|\vx)}{p_{\theta}(\vz|\vx)} d\vz                   \\
                                                & = \e_{\vz \sim q_{\phi}(\vz|\vx)} \left[ \log q_{\phi}(\vz|\vx) - \log p_{\theta}(\vz|\vx) \right]
\end{align}
Substitute $p_{\theta}(\vz|\vx) = \frac{p_{\theta}(\vx|\vz) p(\vz)}{p(\vx)}$:
\begin{align}
     & \KL{q_{\phi}(\vz|\vx)}{p_{\theta}(\vz|\vx)} \nonumber                                                                                      \\
     & \quad \quad = \e_{\vz \sim q_{\phi}(\vz|\vx)} \left[ \log q_{\phi}(\vz|\vx) - \log p_{\theta}(\vx|\vz) - \log p(\vz) + \log p(\vx) \right]
\end{align}
Since $\log p(\vx)$ does not depend on $\vz$, the expectation is just $\log p(\vx)$:
\begin{align}
     & \KL{q_{\phi}(\vz|\vx)}{p_{\theta}(\vz|\vx)} \nonumber                                                                                                                                     \\
     & \quad \quad = \e_{\vz \sim q_{\phi}(\vz|\vx)} \left[ \log q_{\phi}(\vz|\vx) - \log p(\vz) \right] - \e_{\vz \sim q_{\phi}(\vz|\vx)} \left[ \log p_{\theta}(\vx|\vz) \right] + \log p(\vx)
\end{align}
The first term is the KL divergence between the approximate posterior and the prior:
\begin{align}
    \KL{q_{\phi}(\vz|\vx)}{p(\vz)} = \e_{\vz \sim q_{\phi}(\vz|\vx)} \left[ \log q_{\phi}(\vz|\vx) - \log p(\vz) \right]
\end{align}
Substituting this back into the equation of $\KL{q_{\phi}(\vz|\vx)}{p(\vz)}$ gives:
\begin{align}
     & \KL{q_{\phi}(\vz|\vx)}{p_{\theta}(\vz|\vx)} \nonumber                                                                                \\
     & \quad \quad = \KL{q_{\phi}(\vz|\vx)}{p(\vz)} - \e_{\vz \sim q_{\phi}(\vz|\vx)} \left[ \log p_{\theta}(\vx|\vz) \right] + \log p(\vx)
\end{align}
Rearranging for $\log p(\vx)$:
\begin{align}
     & \log p(\vx) \nonumber                                                                                                                                                \\
     & \quad \quad = \e_{\vz \sim q_{\phi}(\vz|\vx)} \left[ \log p_{\theta}(\vx|\vz) \right] - \KL{q_{\phi}(\vz|\vx)}{p(\vz)} + \KL{q_{\phi}(\vz|\vx)}{p_{\theta}(\vz|\vx)}
\end{align}
Since KL divergence is always non-negative ($\KL{q}{p} \ge 0$), we have:
\begin{align}
    \log p(\vx) \ge \e_{\vz \sim q_{\phi}(\vz|\vx)} \left[ \log p_{\theta}(\vx|\vz) \right] - \KL{q_{\phi}(\vz|\vx)}{p(\vz)}
\end{align}
This right-hand side is the Evidence Lower Bound (ELBO), often denoted $\mathcal{L}(\theta, \phi; \vx)$:
\begin{align}
    \mathcal{L}(\theta, \phi; \vx) = \underbrace{\e_{\vz \sim q_{\phi}(\vz|\vx)} \left[ \log p_{\theta}(\vx|\vz) \right]}_{\text{Reconstruction Term}} - \underbrace{\KL{q_{\phi}(\vz|\vx)}{p(\vz)}}_{\substack{\text{KL Divergence /} \\ \text{Regularization Term}}}
    \label{eq:elbointro}
\end{align}
Maximizing the ELBO with respect to parameters $\theta$ and $\phi$ serves two purposes:

1. It pushes $\log p(\vx)$ higher (our original goal).

2. It minimizes the KL divergence between the approximate posterior $q_{\phi}(\vz|\vx)$ and the true posterior $p_{\theta}(\vz|\vx)$ (because $\log p(\vx)$ is fixed for a given $\vx$, and the ELBO is $\log p(\vx) - \KL{q_{\phi}(\vz|\vx)}{p_{\theta}(\vz|\vx)}$).

To making things mathematically explicit, let us take a look at the the formula
of ELBO \eqref{eq:elbointro}. The KL divergence term measures how much the
approximate posterior (learned by the encoder) deviates from the prior
$p(\vz)$. Specifically, when
\begin{align}
    q_{\phi}(\vz|\vx) = \Ndist(\vz; \vmu_{\phi}(\vx), \text{diag}(\vsigma^2_{\phi}(\vx))), \quad p(\vz) = \Ndist(\vz; \mathbf{0}, \mI)
\end{align}
this KL divergence has a convenient analytical solution:
\begin{align}
    \KL{q_{\phi}(\vz|\vx)}{p(\vz)} = \frac{1}{2} \sum_{j=1}^{N} \left( \mu_{j, \phi}^2(\vx) + \sigma_{j, \phi}^2(\vx) - \log(\sigma_{j, \phi}^2(\vx)) - 1 \right)
    \label{eq:kl_gaussian}
\end{align}
To see this, the general KL divergence between $Q = \Ndist(\vmu_Q, \mSigma_Q)$ and $P = \Ndist(\vmu_P, \mSigma_P)$ is
\begin{align}
    \frac{1}{2} \left( \text{Tr}(\mSigma_P^{-1} \mSigma_Q) + (\vmu_P - \vmu_Q)^T \mSigma_P^{-1} (\vmu_P - \vmu_Q) - N + \log \frac{|\mSigma_P|}{|\mSigma_Q|} \right).
\end{align}
Plugging in $\vmu_Q = \vmu_{\phi}(\vx)$, $\mSigma_Q = \text{diag}(\vsigma^2_{\phi}(\vx))$, $\vmu_P = \mathbf{0}$, and $\mSigma_P = \mI$ leads to the simplified formula above.

On the other hand, the reconstruction term usually depends on whether the data
is binary or continuous. For binary data such as MNIST digits, we assume each
pixel $x_i$ is a Bernoulli random variable. The decoder network (parameterized
by $\theta$) takes $\vz$ and outputs a vector $\boldsymbol{p}_{\theta}(\vz)$,
where each element $p_{i, \theta}(\vz)$ is the probability that pixel $x_i$ is
1.
\begin{align} \label{eq:pzbernoulli}
    p_{\theta}(\vx|\vz) = \prod_{i=1}^{N} p_{i, \theta}(\vz)^{x_i} (1 - p_{i, \theta}(\vz))^{1-x_i}
\end{align}
where $N$ is the dimensionality of $\vx$.
The log-likelihood is:
\begin{align}
    \log p_{\theta}(\vx|\vz) = \sum_{i=1}^{N} \left[ x_i \log p_{i, \theta}(\vz) + (1 - x_i) \log(1 - p_{i, \theta}(\vz)) \right]
\end{align}
This is the negative binary cross-entropy between the input $\vx$ and the reconstructed probabilities $\boldsymbol{p}_{\theta}(\vz)$. The reconstruction term becomes:
\begin{align}
    \e_{\vz \sim q_{\phi}(\vz|\vx)} \left[ \sum_{i=1}^{N} \left( x_i \log p_{i, \theta}(\vz) + (1 - x_i) \log(1 - p_{i, \theta}(\vz)) \right) \right]
\end{align}

For continuous data, we assume each dimension $x_i$ is Gaussian. The decoder
network takes $\vz$ and outputs the mean of the Gaussian $\vmu_{\theta}(\vz)$.
We often assume a fixed variance, say $\sigma^2 \mI$, for simplicity (e.g.,
$\sigma^2=1$), or the decoder could also output the variance. Assuming fixed
variance $\sigma^2$:
\begin{align}
    p_{\theta}(\vx|\vz) = \prod_{i=1}^{N} \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi \sigma^2}} \exp\left(-\frac{(x_i - \mu_{i, \theta}(\vz))^2}{2 \sigma^2}\right)
\end{align}
The log-likelihood is:
\begin{align} \label{eq:pzcontinuous}
    \log p_{\theta}(\vx|\vz) & = \sum_{i=1}^{N} \left[ -\frac{1}{2} \log(2\pi \sigma^2) - \frac{(x_i - \mu_{i, \theta}(\vz))^2}{2 \sigma^2} \right] \\
                             & = -\frac{N}{2} \log(2\pi \sigma^2) - \frac{1}{2\sigma^2} \sum_{i=1}^{N} (x_i - \mu_{i, \theta}(\vz))^2               \\
                             & = -\frac{N}{2} \log(2\pi \sigma^2) - \frac{1}{2\sigma^2} ||\vx - \vmu_{\theta}(\vz)||^2
\end{align}
This is proportional to the negative squared error between the input $\vx$ and the reconstructed mean $\vmu_{\theta}(\vz)$. The reconstruction term becomes:
\begin{align}
    \e_{\vz \sim q_{\phi}(\vz|\vx)} \left[ -\frac{N}{2} \log(2\pi \sigma^2) - \frac{1}{2\sigma^2} ||\vx - \vmu_{\theta}(\vz)||^2 \right]
\end{align}
Since the constant term doesn't affect optimization w.r.t $\theta, \phi$, maximizing this term is equivalent to minimizing the expected squared error: $\e_{\vz \sim q_{\phi}(\vz|\vx)} [||\vx - \vmu_{\theta}(\vz)||^2]$.

After clearing things up, let us go back to \eqref{eq:elbointro}. There's a
problem with the reconstruction term $\e_{\vz \sim q_{\phi}(\vz|\vx)} [\dots]$.
We need to compute its gradient with respect to $\phi$. However, the
expectation is taken over a distribution $q_{\phi}$ whose parameters
($\vmu_{\phi}, \vsigma^2_{\phi}$) depend on $\phi$. Computationally, standard
backpropagation cannot handle gradients through the sampling process $\vz \sim
    q_{\phi}(\vz|\vx)$.

The famous reparameterization trick (\cite{kingma2013auto}) solves this (which
is also applicable to VarFA). For distributions like the Gaussian, we can
rewrite the sampling process. Instead of sampling $\vz$ directly from
$\Ndist(\vmu_{\phi}(\vx), \text{diag}(\vsigma^2_{\phi}(\vx)))$, we first sample
a noise vector $\vepsilon$ from a fixed, simple distribution, typically a
standard normal: $\vepsilon \sim \Ndist(\mathbf{0}, \mI)$. Then we can
transform this noise using the parameters from the encoder:
\begin{align}
    \vz = \vmu_{\phi}(\vx) + \vsigma_{\phi}(\vx) \odot \vepsilon
    \label{eq:reparam}
\end{align}
where $\odot$ denotes the element-wise product (Hadamard product), and $\vsigma_{\phi}(\vx)$ is the vector of standard deviations (square root of the variances $\vsigma^2_{\phi}(\vx)$). Note that the encoder usually outputs $\log \vsigma^2_{\phi}(\vx)$, so $\vsigma_{\phi}(\vx) = \exp(0.5 \times \log \vsigma^2_{\phi}(\vx))$.

Now, $\vz$ still follows the desired distribution $q_{\phi}(\vz|\vx)$, but the
stochasticity comes from $\vepsilon$, which is independent of $\phi$. The
dependence on $\phi$ is now deterministic through $\vmu_{\phi}(\vx)$ and
$\vsigma_{\phi}(\vx)$.

We can rewrite the reconstruction term in \eqref{eq:elbointro} using this
trick:
\begin{align}
    \e_{\vz \sim q_{\phi}(\vz|\vx)} \left[ \log p_{\theta}(\vx|\vz) \right] = \e_{\vepsilon \sim \Ndist(\mathbf{0}, \mI)} \left[ \log p_{\theta}(\vx|\vz = \vmu_{\phi}(\vx) + \vsigma_{\phi}(\vx) \odot \vepsilon) \right]
\end{align}
In practice, we approximate this expectation using Monte Carlo sampling, typically with just one sample ($L=1$) of $\vepsilon$ per data point $\vx$ per training step:
\begin{align}
    \log p_{\theta}(\vx|\vz^{(i, l)}) \quad \text{where} \quad \vz^{(i, l)} = \vmu_{\phi}(\vx^{(i)}) +  \vsigma_{\phi}(\vx^{(i)}) \odot \vepsilon^{(l)}  \quad \text{and} \quad \vepsilon^{(l)} \sim \Ndist(\mathbf{0}, \mI)
\end{align}
Here we used superscript to distinguish sampled data points from general variables and coordinates. Combining everything, the objective function we want to maximize (or minimize its negative) for a single data point $\vx$, using the reparameterization trick \eqref{eq:reparam} and the analytical KL divergence \eqref{eq:kl_gaussian}, is approximated as:
\begin{align}
    \mathcal{L}(\theta, \phi; \vx) \approx \log p_{\theta}(\vx|\vz = \vmu_{\phi}(\vx) & +  \vsigma_{\phi}(\vx) \odot \vepsilon ) \nonumber                                                                           \\
    -                                                                                 & \frac{1}{2} \sum_{j=1}^{N} \left( \mu_{j, \phi}^2(\vx) + \sigma_{j, \phi}^2(\vx) - \log(\sigma_{j, \phi}^2(\vx)) - 1 \right)
    \label{eq:final_elbo_approx}
\end{align}
where $\vepsilon \sim \Ndist(\mathbf{0}, \mI)$ is sampled once.

We typically minimize the \emph{negative} ELBO averaged over a mini-batch of
$M$ data points:
\begin{align}
    \text{Loss} & = -\frac{1}{M} \sum_{i=1}^M \mathcal{L}(\theta, \phi; \vx^{(i)})                                                                                                                                                                                                                                               \\
                & \approx \frac{1}{M} \sum_{i=1}^M \left[ \underbrace{-\log p_{\theta}(\vx^{(i)}|\vz^{(i)})}_{\text{Reconstruction Loss}} + \underbrace{\frac{1}{2} \sum_{j=1}^{N} \left( \mu_{j, \phi}^2(\vx^{(i)}) + \sigma_{j, \phi}^2(\vx^{(i)}) - \log(\sigma_{j, \phi}^2(\vx^{(i)})) - 1 \right)}_{\text{KL Loss}} \right]
    \label{eq:final_loss}
\end{align}
where $\vz^{(i)} = \vmu_{\phi}(\vx^{(i)}) +  \vsigma_{\phi}(\vx^{(i)}) \odot \vepsilon^{(i)}$ with $\vepsilon^{(i)} \sim \Ndist(\mathbf{0}, \mI)$.

The term $-\log p_{\theta}(\vx^{(i)}|\vz^{(i)})$ is the reconstruction loss
(e.g., binary cross-entropy or mean squared error, depending on the data type
and choice of $p_{\theta}$).

At this point, we have broken down the VAE algorithm into as many details as
possible for mathematical clarity. Let us end this section with the formal
procedure of the training process and data generation after the training is
done, so that the readers know what exactly is being computed in each step.

\textbf{Training Procedure of VAE}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Initialize encoder parameters $\phi$ and decoder parameters $\theta$.
    \item Repeat until convergence:
          \begin{enumerate}
              \item Sample a mini-batch of data $\{\vx^{(1)}, ..., \vx^{(M)}\}$.
              \item For each $\vx^{(i)}$ in the mini-batch:
                    \begin{enumerate}
                        \item Pass $\vx^{(i)}$ through the encoder network to get $\vmu_{\phi}(\vx^{(i)})$
                              and $\log \vsigma^2_{\phi}(\vx^{(i)})$.
                        \item Calculate $\vsigma_{\phi}(\vx^{(i)}) = \exp(0.5 \times \log
                                  \vsigma^2_{\phi}(\vx^{(i)}))$.
                        \item Sample $\vepsilon^{(i)} \sim \Ndist(\mathbf{0}, \mI)$.
                        \item Compute the latent variable sample: $\vz^{(i)} = \vmu_{\phi}(\vx^{(i)}) +
                                  \vsigma_{\phi}(\vx^{(i)}) \odot \vepsilon^{(i)}$.
                        \item Pass $\vz^{(i)}$ through the decoder network to get the parameters of
                              $p_{\theta}(\vx|\vz^{(i)})$ (e.g., $\boldsymbol{p}_{\theta}(\vz^{(i)})$ for
                              Bernoulli, or $\vmu_{\theta}(\vz^{(i)})$ for Gaussian).
                        \item Calculate the reconstruction loss term $-\log p_{\theta}(\vx^{(i)}|\vz^{(i)})$.
                        \item Calculate the KL divergence term $\KL{q_{\phi}(\vz|\vx^{(i)})}{p(\vz)}$ using
                              the analytical formula (\ref{eq:kl_gaussian}) with $\vmu_{\phi}(\vx^{(i)})$ and
                              $\vsigma^2_{\phi}(\vx^{(i)})$.
                    \end{enumerate}
              \item Compute the average loss over the mini-batch (Negative ELBO,
                    \eqref{eq:final_loss}).
              \item Compute gradients of the loss with respect to $\phi$ and $\theta$ using back
                    propagation (enabled by the reparameterization trick).
              \item Update $\phi$ and $\theta$ using an optimizer (e.g., Adam, SGD).
          \end{enumerate}
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Data Generation Procedure after Training}

Once the VAE is trained, we can generate new data points $\vx_{new}$ that
resemble the training data:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Sample a latent vector $\vz_{new}$ from the prior distribution: $\vz_{new} \sim
              p(\vz) = \Ndist(\mathbf{0}, \mI)$.
    \item Pass $\vz_{new}$ through the trained decoder network to obtain the parameters
          of $p_{\theta}(\vx|\vz_{new})$.
    \item Sample a new data point $\vx_{new}$ from this distribution
          $p_{\theta}(\vx|\vz_{new})$. (For example, if $p_{\theta}$ is Gaussian with
          mean $\vmu_{\theta}(\vz_{new})$ and fixed variance $\sigma^2$, then $\vx_{new}
              \sim \Ndist(\vmu_{\theta}(\vz_{new}), \sigma^2\mI)$). Often, for image
          generation, people just use the mean output of the decoder (e.g.,
          $\boldsymbol{p}_{\theta}(\vz_{new})$ or $\vmu_{\theta}(\vz_{new})$) directly as
          the generated sample, rather than sampling from the output distribution.
\end{enumerate}
\subsection{The High-Level View of VarFA based on VAE}
After going through VAE thoroughly, we can end the introduction of chapter 4
with a short section highlighting the difference between VarFA and VAE. The
mathematical deduction in the previous section, along with the high-level
description provided in this section should prepare us well for the content in
chapter 4. Generally speaking, VarFA can be applied in all scenarios where both
dimension reduction and interoperability are necessary. In our case, we choose
education as the main field of application because the necessity and advantage
of interpretable models and dimension reduction are more or less self-evident.

In our work, VarFA starts with two more interpretable assumptions compared to
VAE:

1. The students' overall performance follow a FA model.

2. The students' latent skills follow a multi-dimensional Gaussian distribution. (Like we mentioned previously, we assume everyone performs around their average level, with over low and over high performance being exponentially rare.)

With these two assumptions, VarFA uses a similar encoder-decoder architecture
as VAE, and VarFA can also be trained using the reparameterization trick
efficiently based on a modified ELBO (details in chapter 4). After the training
is complete, VarFA takes students' performance record and determines their
latent skills during the encoder phase. This step is much more interpretable
than the usual VAE and much more similar to the natural thinking process of
humans. For instance, if a student performs well in math and physics, VarFA
will assign a high competence factor for their quantitative reasoning skills.
This is crucial for machine learning in education, as prediction is not the end
goal, but the reflection and feedback to students.

In the decoder phase, given students' latent skills, questions' difficulty and
their correlations to the skills, VarFA is able to predict students'
performance in a natural, interpretable way. Intuitively speaking, if a student
is good at reading and writing, it is very likely for them to answer an easy
question in the subject of literature. We can use VarFA to track the
performance of students and predict their potential areas for improvement. On
the other hand, VarFA can also be used to spot possible academic dishonesty. A
well-trained VarFA model can detect students who suddenly perform well in a
subject they have been struggling with. This interpretable generative model is
a versatile and useful tool to use in academic environments.

VarFA marks the small but meaningful effort to transfer the power of machine
learning into human learning. As machines learn faster and better, we should
not forget our shared responsibility to prepare our future generations for this
ever-changing world. Because the new science and technology require a longer
study plan than ever, it is imperative to make sure that we use the
advancements in AI to make sure that knowledge is widely accessible, and we are
indeed making this world a better place.
